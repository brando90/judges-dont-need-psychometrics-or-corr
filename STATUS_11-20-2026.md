# Status 11-20-2026: correlation versus loss for judges, what is done and what is not

**Doc link:** <https://github.com/brando90/judges-dont-need-psychometrics-or-corr/blob/main/STATUS_11-20-2026.md>

**TLDR:** The claim the evidence supports today: correlation cannot tell judges apart by rating level, scale or spread; when a judge's output is read as a rating or a distribution, select and report it with a proper scoring rule (ranked probability score, RPS), and keep correlation as a ranking diagnostic. On the historical human ratings the two selection rules are not yet statistically distinguishable. Retrospective data throughout (25 tasks, 75 artifacts, 195 expert ratings), plus synthetic toys.

## Bottom line
1. **Done, and solid:** correlation alone cannot identify a judge's rating distribution (experiments 01, 05); reliability numbers alone cannot either (04).
2. **Human ratings, scalar votes (5 cached frontier judges, 39 open-weight judges):** picking ONE judge is no better than the constant forecast within noise (by correlation 0.180 against 0.184; by CE 0.205 and by RPS 0.207, both worse), and picking by RPS is no better than by CE or by correlation. **Mixing** judges by RPS is the best fitted arm on the frontier pool (RPS 0.131 against constant 0.184). The 39 open-weight scalar judges lose to the constant under every method.
3. **Human ratings, native probabilities (experiments 09, 10):** asking the model for a six-category probability beats the old three-vote scalar pool on CE (frontier 5.49 to 1.42 nats, constant 1.93; open-weight 3.48 to 1.63, constant 1.89), and loss-trained fits have lower measured losses than correlation-trained fits. Exploratory intervals; the prompt-search comparisons are uncertain.
4. **Toy (experiment 11):** with a simple calibrator after a score, the gap to end-to-end CE training is a few percent of the irreducible loss at large sample size. A flexible (nonlinear) calibrator closes most of it when the score is good (experiment 17, below).
5. **Rater severity (experiment 15):** constant per-rater offsets are about 0.1 rating points (artifact SD 1.44 against rater SD 0.13) and explain about 2% of within-artifact variance. The best judge and the RPS ranking do not change when they are removed.
6. **Not done:** prospective test on fresh ratings (Version 27, 11-17-2026, says it awaits fresh human ratings in trustworthy-theorem-judges experiment 09; I did not re-check that repo today), Claude, Gemini and Grok judges with native probabilities, prompts-versus-weights comparison after the workshop deadline (Sanmi's wording).

## Experiment 17 (flexible calibrator, toy, $0)
Pre-registered before the sweep, then run on 4,400 synthetic cells (4 scenarios, n from 4 to 2,048, 100 seeds), $0. Results: [experiment 17](experiments/17_flexible_scalar_calibrator_vs_1d_bottleneck_claim_start_20Nov2026/results.md).
- With the true mean rating as the score, a flexible calibrator matches or beats end-to-end CE training (RPS gap -0.0004 to -0.0039 at n >= 512). One number is not a representational bottleneck in these toys.
- With a Pearson-trained score, the flexible calibrator closes the gap to CE at n >= 1,024 in scenarios B, C, D, but not in A (+0.0052 RPS at n = 2,048): the gap is the quality of the learned score, not its one-dimensional shape.
- Consequence: Version 27's "1D bottleneck" wording should become "a score trained to track the mean can omit information about spread". Caveat: the toy input is a single number, so it cannot test a many-dimensional judge squeezed to one number.
