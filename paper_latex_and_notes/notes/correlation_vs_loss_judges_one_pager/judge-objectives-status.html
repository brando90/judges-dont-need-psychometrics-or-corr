Correlation vs loss for training LLM judges

Should we train, prompt, harness, or calibrate a large language model (LLM) judge by correlation with human ratings, or by a strictly proper scoring rule (ranked probability score, RPS, or cross-entropy, CE) against the conditional 0–5 (or 0–4 / 1–5) rating distribution? Here is the full evidence — incorporating Sanmi Koyejo's 11-16 feedback, the deposited Stanford preprint (DOI: 10.25740/bh388th6008, exp 27), frontier homework break-even (exp 26), ChaosNLI + DICES frontier transforms (exp 28), and all 1,401/1,401 matched four-objective (CE, RPS, MSE, Pearson) plus K=2 shape-control LoRA runs across Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (921) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (480) on ChaosNLI, DICES-350/990, and nvidia/HelpSteer2 (exp 29 complete).

Bottom line (narrow supported framing)

  • Core principle: Correlation measures linear or rank association with human mean ratings and cannot certify level, scale, or conditional spread. When a judge's output is read as a rating or distribution, fit, select, or calibrate with a strictly proper scoring rule like ranked probability score (RPS) (range 0–1, lower is better) and retain correlation as a ranking diagnostic. Psychometric tools remain complementary (Lin 1989 concordance, G-theory design allocation).
  • In theory, proper losses identify the distribution while correlation ties (exp 01): Correlation cannot separate candidate distributions with identical conditional means; single-rating CE selects the oracle in 249/256 replications.
  • On historical 3-sample scalar votes, empty bins break CE and single-judge selection favored correlation — fixed by native probabilities and RPS (exps 02, 12, 14 → 09, 10): On 3-sample histograms, empty bins hit the 10−6 probability floor (CE 5.49 and 3.48 nats vs 1.93/1.89 constant), so 5-frontier mixtures were unresolved on RPS (+0.0056 [−0.0059, +0.0181]) and CE single-judge selection lost (−0.025 in exp 12, −0.032 in exp 14). Switching to native 6-category probabilities (exps 09 & 10) drops held-out CE below the constant baseline (1.416 nats frontier Native-15, 1.629/1.606 nats open-weight) and flips both mixture and single-judge selection: Native-15 CE-mix (0.114 RPS) beats Pearson-mix (0.191) by +0.0775 [+0.0437, +0.1135]; Native-15 train-CE (0.125) and train-RPS (0.136) single judges both beat train-Pearson (0.143, or 0.185 in Combined-20). Even on Hist-5 scalars, the ④ RPS-trained mixture reaches 0.1350 RPS.
  • Weights & score-then-calibrate strongly favor proper loss, while coarse discrete single-prompt selection is noisy (exps 09 & 10): End-to-end PyTorch LoRA weights favor CE over Pearson (+0.1344 [+0.0495, +0.2220] RPS on Qwen3-4B; +0.2088 [+0.0957, +0.3037] on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B), and adding a 12-param CE calibration head atop Pearson features ("score-then-calibrate", Sanmi's suggested baseline) recovers strong accuracy (0.1412 RPS / 1.593 nats on Qwen3-4B; 0.1301 RPS on frontier 48-wt head), while CE 48-wt heads beat Pearson from 5 training families (~52 ratings). Conversely, uncalibrated single-prompt/harness selection on 3–5 validation families is noisy (−0.0261 exp 09 meta-prompt; +0.0418 / −0.0060 exp 10 discrete search), though harness mixtures (+0.0511 [+0.0222, +0.0833]) and two-step obligation auditing (0.114–0.160 RPS) help clearly.
  • Public benchmarks & HelpSteer2 LoRA weight scaling across budgets and model families (exps 23, 24, 25): On prompt-disjoint public benchmarks (exp 23: KonIQ 2,015 test images / 215,415 votes, SummEval 40 test articles / 1,920 grades), discrete CE clears raw Pearson and uniform from 12 crowd grades on KonIQ (24 after training-draw sensitivity; 0.1068 vs 0.3072 RPS at 6,144 grades) while unseen SummEval categories punish unsmoothed CE until Jeffreys pseudocounts (≥ 0.01) flip the margin, and 4-bin train-only proper-loss calibration of Pearson matches or beats discrete CE on both (0.0543 and 0.0922 RPS). When fine-tuning top-4-block LoRA weights (rank = 8) with matched AdamW grids, S_val-RPS selection, and disjoint S_cal 7-parameter CE calibration on nvidia/HelpSteer2 (1,038 test responses, 519 prompts, 3,610 votes; human leave-one-vote-out RPS 0.14567), uncalibrated raw Pearson LoRA loses on mean RPS at every budget and in every draw at max budget (20/20 Qwen3-4B draws across all Exp 25 budgets B = 512..8,192 and 8/10 at B = 16, 9/10 at B = 32, 10/10 for B = 64..2,048 in Exp 24, +0.01971 [+0.01509, +0.02432] RPS at B = 8,192; 10/10 Llama-3.1-8B draws for B = 64..2,048, +0.03379 [+0.01477, +0.05280] RPS at B = 2,048, crossed p = 0.0002 at both max budgets). After adding identical 7-parameter CE calibration on S_cal, Qwen3-4B CE LoRA stays lower on mean RPS for B_total ≥ 64 (6/8 Exp 24 budgets and all 5/5 Exp 25 budgets B = 512..8,192; 0.15200 vs 0.15309 at B = 2,048, 9/10 draws, cond. sim. p = 0.0177 in exp 24; 0.15080 at B = 8,192 in exp 25 beating base_calibrated_all_dev 0.15481 at crossed p = 0.0002), while growing N_cal → 2,048 closes the calibrated RPS gap (+0.00009 [−0.00117, +0.00136], 11/20 draws on RPS, 16/20 on CE and total variation distance (TVD)); on weaker Llama-3.1-8B (base_calibrated_all_dev 0.16186), calibrated RPS differences remain indistinguishable across B_total = 16..2,048 (0.16005 vs 0.15893 at 2,048, crossed p = 0.8533).
  • Frontier break-even & four-objective LoRA shape control (exps 26, 28, 29): On 160 fresh homework answers scored by five frontier models (exp 26, $1.34), discrete CE-fit beats Pearson-fit in 35/35 judge–budget pairs (GPT-6 Astra and Kimi K3 pass 256→8). Across 2,350 ChaosNLI + DICES items × 5 frontier judges (exp 28, ~$19.69), CE-fit clears Pearson-fit after 4–8 (d=1) or 10–20 (d=5) ratings in 25/25 dataset–judge pairs (Pearson+cal wins DICES from 64). Setup and notation

①Validation-selected correlation: choose ridge/prompt/harness/checkpoint by correlation on validation data (run on synthetic ratings in exp 08 and retrospective human ratings in exps 09 & 10). ②Train-correlation judge: fit mixture weights, softmax/hidden-state heads, LoRA weights, or pick one base judge on training tasks to maximize Pearson or Spearman with mean human ratings. ③Validation-selected proper loss: tune ridge/prompt/harness/checkpoint/shrinkage by validation RPS or CE (run in synthetic exp 08 and human-rating exps 09 & 10). ④Train proper-loss judge: fit the same model class (mixture, single judge, 48–78 wt softmax head, or PyTorch LoRA weights) on training tasks to minimize CE or RPS against individual human ratings.

Both kinds of judge are then scored with the same metrics on the same held-out test task families or tasks. Method names describe how a judge was trained or selected, never the metric it is scored with.

What "judge" and "training" mean across historical vs new human-rating experiments. In exps 02, 11, 12 and 14, a base judge formed a 3-sample empirical histogram over integer 0–5 scores (leaving empty bins at 10−6), and optimization only tuned simplex weights or picked one base judge. In exps 09 and 10, judges output native six-category probability vectors (verbalized JSON probabilities and conditional digit logprobs), and optimization spans five model-modification axes: (a) multi-model & blind iterative prompt search (P0..P5 plus agentic rubric rewriting on held-out families), (b) scoring harnesses (H1..H6, abstract syntax tree (AST) obligation extraction, two-step decompose-then-aggregate, temperature calibration, thinking mode), (c) 48-parameter softmax feature heads across 3..15 task families, (d) end-to-end PyTorch low-rank adaptation (LoRA) transformer backpropagation (1.85M–2.91M params), and (e) two-stage score-then-calibrate heads (Pearson + 12-param CE head).

RPS
Ranked probability score of the judge's predicted rating distribution against each human rating: (1/5) Σk=0..4 (Q(k) − 1{rating ≤ k})², averaged over ratings and artifacts. Range 0 to 1, lower is better. The primary metric. The appendix explains it with examples.
CE
Cross-entropy (log loss): average −ln q(rating), in nats, lower is better.
TVD
Total variation distance to each artifact's human rating histogram, 0 to 1, lower is better.
Intervals
95% intervals: 10,000 task/family bootstrap draws (exps 10, 11, 12, 14; exp 02 uses 998/1,000 valid family refits); exact 219 task-family sign-flip tests & 10,000-draw family bootstrap (exp 09); 250-split conditional Monte Carlo intervals on fixed data in exp 10 M3 learning curve; 4,000 item/article bootstrap draws (exp 23); 4,000 conditional prompt-clustered and two-way crossed draw × prompt bootstrap draws (exps 24 & 25); 10,000 seed draws for synthetic exps 06–08 and 13.

The unpenalized RPS and correlation objectives are bounded and nonconstant, so they cannot be globally convex in W. This argument does not apply directly after adding the unbounded ridge penalty; the penalized fits were not certified globally optimal. Unclipped softmax CE with ridge is convex. Experiment 07 (Figure 7) logged convergence. CE, RPS, Kendall and the calibrated head converge in 97–100% of fits. Pearson-trained fits never converge within 400 iterations from 32 training tasks on, and 91% still hit a 2,000-iteration budget. Best-of-5 starts with the larger budget improve their penalized training objective, but every held-out test RPS crossover stays the same at the tested sizes. This does not establish independence from the choice of optimizer.

Notation: rating distributions and what each objective can learn

Bin(n, θ)
Binomial: n trials, success probability θ. Pr[R = r] = C(n, r) θr (1 − θ)n−r; mean nθ; variance nθ(1 − θ).
BetaBin(n, α, β)
Beta-binomial: draw θ ~ Beta(α, β), then R ~ Bin(n, θ). With α = μκ and β = (1 − μ)κ: mean nμ, variance nμ(1 − μ)(κ + n)/(κ + 1). κ sets rater disagreement; small κ splits raters toward 0 and 5.
Cat(p0, …, p5)
A general distribution over the six ratings: five free probabilities.
Data
An item's ratings r1, …, rm are independent draws, with counts nk = #{i : ri = k}.

Judge model in exp 13: qW(r | x) = softmax(W⊤φ(x))r, with φ(x) = 13 cosine and sine features of x and W a 13 × 6 matrix. Every objective fits this same family; only the objective differs.

General lesson. For a one-parameter family such as Bin(n, θ), the mean is a sufficient statistic, so a correct mean gives a correct distribution. We do not assume human ratings belong to a known one-parameter family. Disagreement (spread, raters split between 0 and 5) is extra information that only the full rating counts carry, and only proper losses use it. Via E[R] = Σj Sj, mean-based objectives compress those counts to one number per item.

Held-out test RPS on human ratings: correlation-trained vs loss-trained

Data
Each judge is trained on the human ratings of training tasks, then scored on held-out test tasks it never saw during training (exps 12, 14: 1,000 random splits of 20 training and 5 test tasks; exp 02: 5-fold task-family splits, repeated over 5 partitions). These 75 artifacts were used in earlier studies, so this is retrospective, not a fresh test set.
Plotted
Test RPS of the correlation-trained judge (②) minus test RPS of the loss-trained judge (④), both on the same test artifacts. Both numbers are the same metric, RPS, in the same units. "Correlation-trained" and "loss-trained" describe how each judge was built, not how it is scored: like two students who studied in different ways taking the same exam. Example, the "Exp 12 · 5 frontier judges" mixture row: judge ② has test RPS 0.1419 and judge ④ has 0.1362, so the difference is about +0.006 (reported +0.0056 before rounding).
Reading
+0.05 means the correlation-trained judge's test RPS is 0.05 higher (worse) than the loss-trained judge's; −0.05 means 0.05 lower (better). For scale, typical test RPS values are 0.14 to 0.33 (right-hand column), and predicting the training tasks' rating histogram for every artifact gives 0.184.

In response to Sanmi's 11-16 feedback (asking to add an RPS-selected condition, test score-then-calibrate baselines, and compare prompts/harnesses vs weights) and Nicole's question on generalizability theory (G-theory) / decision studies (D-studies), three follow-ups resolve the mechanism behind the historical 3-sample tie:

  • Why empty bins broke historical CE, and how native 6-category probabilities fix it: In historical pools (exps 02, 12, 14), 3 integer samples guaranteed at least 3 empty bins per artifact, causing 1e-6 floor penalties (13.8 nats/miss) that punished single-judge CE selection (0.205 RPS). Exp 13 · setup (synthetic toy that works by construction)

Each synthetic task has one feature x. Human ratings on 0–5 follow a beta-binomial whose mean and disagreement both depend on x. Every judge is the same flexible model of x; only the objective used to fit it differs. Each judge is trained on n tasks with 3 ratings each, then scored on 500 fresh test tasks against the true rating distribution.

Tasks
x \sim \mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]
Human ratings
R \mid x \sim \mathrm{BetaBin}\big(5,\ \mu(x)\kappa(x),\ (1-\mu(x))\kappa(x)\big),\qquad \mathbb{E}[R\mid x]=5\mu(x),\qquad \mathrm{Var}[R\mid x]=5\mu(x)\big(1-\mu(x)\big)\frac{\kappa(x)+5}{\kappa(x)+1}
Disagreement
\kappa(x)=\exp\big(1.5+2.2\cos(1.5\pi x)\big)\in[0.50,\ 40.4]. Small κ splits raters toward 0 and 5; large κ means raters agree.
Scenario A: "same mean"
\mu_A(x)=0.5+0.08\,x \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[R\mid x]=2.5+0.4\,x\in[2.1,\ 2.9]. The mean moves at most 0.4 rating points from 2.5, while at μ = 0.5 the variance ranges from 1.37 (κ ≈ 40, raters agree) to 4.59 (κ ≈ 0.50, raters split). Almost all differences between tasks are in disagreement, not in the mean.
Scenario B
\mu_B(x)=0.1+0.8\left(\tfrac{x+1}{2}\right)^2 \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[R\mid x] rises monotonically from 0.5 to 4.5, and κ(x) varies as above.
Training data
n tasks x_1,\dots,x_n, each with 3 ratings; empirical histogram \hat p_i(r)=\tfrac13\,\#\{m: r_{im}=r\}, its cumulative distribution function (CDF) \hat P_i(j) and mean \hat m_i.
Judge family
q_W(r\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\big(W^\top\phi(x)\big)_r,\quad \phi(x)=\big(1,\cos\pi x,\sin\pi x,\dots,\cos 6\pi x,\sin 6\pi x\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{13},\quad W\in\mathbb{R}^{13\times 6},\quad m_W(x)=\textstyle\sum_r r\,q_W(r\mid x)
CE-trained (④)
\min_W\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{5}\hat p_i(r)\,\big[-\log q_W(r\mid x_i)\big]+\frac1n\|W\|^2
RPS-trained
\min_W\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac15\sum_{j=0}^{4}\big(Q_W(j\mid x_i)-\hat P_i(j)\big)^2+\frac1n\|W\|^2
Pearson-trained (②)
\max_W\ \operatorname{corr}_{i=1..n}\big(m_W(x_i),\ \hat m_i\big)-\frac1n\|W\|^2
Pearson constrains only the mean m_W(x), and only up to an increasing affine map. The rest of the distribution's shape is left to the starting point (the average training rating histogram) and the ridge penalty.
Pearson + calibrated head
A two-stage "score, then calibrate" judge. Stage 1: train W by Pearson as above, giving one score per task, s(x)=m_W(x). Stage 2: standardize it on the training tasks, z(x)=(s(x)-\bar s)/\mathrm{sd}(s), and fit a small six-way softmax on that single number,
q_{\text{head}}(r\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\big(a_r+b_r\,z(x)\big),\qquad (a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^6\times\mathbb{R}^6\ \text{fitted by CE with ridge }(1/n)\|(a,b)\|_2^2.
"Calibrated" means the head turns the score into probabilities for each rating that match observed rating frequencies. Even though b=0 nests the constant histogram, fitting Stage 1 without regularizing toward the mean lets overfit z(x) drive extreme Stage 2 logits at n\le 32 (RPS excess 0.086–0.248) when 1/n ridge is not separately tuned on (a,b), and because the head sees only one number per task, it cannot recover disagreement that the score does not carry. As tested directly in exp 11 below, cross-fitting Stage 1 + tuning slope ridge c_b\in\{0.01..10^8\} eliminates the small-n spike by n = 32 yet still hits the 1D bottleneck whenever disagreement varies (A, B).
Test metrics
On 500 fresh tasks x'_t per seed, against the true distribution p: \text{RPS excess}=\frac1{500}\sum_t\frac15\sum_{j=0}^{4}\big(Q(j\mid x'_t)-P(j\mid x'_t)\big)^2 and \text{CE excess}=\frac1{500}\sum_t \mathrm{KL}\big(p(\cdot\mid x'_t)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x'_t)\big). Both are 0 for a perfect judge. The true distribution itself has test RPS 0.187 in scenario A and 0.136 in scenario B. 100 seeds per n.

Exp 06 · does training on a rank correlation, or a Pearson + Spearman mix, fix it?

Complete: 11 training sizes (4 to 2,048 tasks) × 100 seeds × 2 scenarios.

Same judge family, same seeds and the identical training and test tasks as exp 13. Three new training objectives:

  • Spearman-trained: maximizes Spearman between the judge's expected ratings and the training-task mean human ratings.
  • Kendall-trained: maximizes Kendall's tau between the same two quantities.
  • ½ Pearson + ½ Spearman-trained: maximizes the average of the two correlations.

Rank correlations are not differentiable, so training uses smooth surrogates (temperature 0.1 rating points). Refitting CE on the regenerated data reproduces exp 13's saved numbers to 1e-14.

Why test correlation is the wrong primary metric (for reviewer 2)

  1. Even on correlation, training on correlation buys little. At the smaller tested synthetic training sizes, the loss-trained judge has equal or higher held-out test correlation (Figure 6). Correlation-trained judges overtake only with many ratings, and then only slightly, while their rating distributions are far worse (Figure 5).
  2. Correlation cannot see what matters. Maximizing Pearson is the same as minimizing squared error between z-scored judge and human ratings. So Pearson is unchanged by any shift or positive rescaling of the predicted ratings, exactly the bias and scale terms of MSE (derivation), and Spearman by any increasing transform. Both see one number per item, the mean, via E[R] = Σj Sj. So neither identifies scale, signed bias or the full pattern of rater disagreement. Figure 2: correlation ties five different distributions in every replication. Exp 00: a judge that only ever outputs 4 or 5 still has Pearson 0.877, against 0.900 for a faithful judge.
  3. Correlation is not a proper scoring rule. It does not reward reporting the true rating distribution. Its value also depends on how spread out the test items are, not just on the judge, so it cannot be averaged item by item or compared across test sets.
  4. What correlation is still good for. If the only use is ranking items, Spearman is a reasonable secondary diagnostic. To choose or train a judge that must reproduce human ratings, use a proper loss such as RPS, and report correlations alongside it.

Appendix: Pearson correlation written as a squared loss

Let X be the judge's expected rating on a random test artifact, X=\mathbb{E}_{q}[R], and Y the human mean rating on the same artifact.

A. Population form

\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\,\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}

If X and Y are centered (\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0), this simplifies to \rho_{X,Y}=\mathbb{E}[XY]/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}.

B. Sample form over N held-out test artifacts

Stack the judge's expected ratings and the human mean ratings into vectors \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^N, center them, and normalize:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}-\bar{x}\,\mathbf{1},\quad \tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{y}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1},\qquad r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2}=\cos\theta=\hat{\mathbf{x}}^\top\hat{\mathbf{y}},\quad \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2},\ \hat{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2}

Pearson correlation is the cosine of the angle between the centered vectors.

C. Pearson is a squared loss on standardized scores

With z-scores Z_X=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X and Z_Y=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y:

\mathbb{E}\big[(Z_X-Z_Y)^2\big]=2\,(1-\rho_{X,Y}),\qquad \|\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2=2\,(1-r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})

So maximizing Pearson is the same as minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between z-scored judge ratings and z-scored human ratings. Using (1-\rho)^2 instead gives the same optimum, because it is a decreasing function of ρ.

D. What plain MSE keeps and Pearson throws away (Lin 1989 concordance, ICC(C,1) vs ICC(A,1), & many-facet models)

With population standard deviations s_x=\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2} and likewise s_y, the MSE between raw ratings splits exactly into three parts (Lin 1989's concordance correlation identity, matching McGraw & Wong 1996's intraclass correlation split between ICC(C,1) consistency and ICC(A,1) absolute agreement):

\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-y_i)^2=\underbrace{(\bar{x}-\bar{y})^2}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{(s_x-s_y)^2}_{\text{scale mismatch}}+\underbrace{2\,s_x s_y\,(1-r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})}_{\text{the only part Pearson sees}}

Pearson drops the bias and scale terms: any judge \mathbf{x}'=a\,\mathbf{x}+b\,\mathbf{1} with a>0 has exactly the same r. For any nonconstant panel of human scores y on 0–5, a judge that predicts x=0.1+0.08\,y has r = 1 despite compressing every prediction into 0.1–0.5. Its mean squared error depends on the panel's mean and variance. Likewise, generalizability theory (G-theory, exp 04) separates relative repeatability Eρ² from absolute agreement Φ and helps plan #raters × #occasions designs, while ordinal likelihood models with rater facets (Linacre 1989 many-facet Rasch models) are themselves proper-loss judges—our critique targets uncalibrated correlation-only selection, not psychometrics at large.

E. Both see only the mean

Each entry x_i=\mathbb{E}_{q_i}[R]=\sum_{j=0}^{4}S_{ij} compresses the judge's whole distribution over the six ratings into one number, and y_i does the same to the humans' ratings. So even plain MSE identifies only the conditional mean, and Pearson sees less than that. Distributions that share every mean get identical Pearson and MSE (Figure 2). RPS and cross-entropy compare the whole distribution on every artifact.

The identities hold for nonconstant score vectors (positive standard deviations). No particular score distribution is assumed in the identities.

Appendix: the ranked probability score (RPS)

Ranked probability score (RPS) is a standard scoring rule in weather forecasting, introduced by Epstein (1969), but it is uncommon in machine learning. It scores a predicted probability distribution over ordered categories, such as our 0–5 ratings, against the rating a human actually gave.

Definition

For one artifact, the judge outputs probabilities q0, …, q5 over the six ratings. Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is Qj = Pr[rating ≤ j] = q0 + … + qj, and its survival function is Sj = Pr[rating > j] = 1 − Qj. For an observed human rating r:

RPS(q, r) = (1/5) · Σj=0..4 ( Qj − 1{r ≤ j} )² = (1/5) · Σj=0..4 ( Sj − 1{r > j} )²

Threshold j = 5 is skipped because Q5 = 1 for every forecast. Dividing by the five thresholds puts RPS between 0 (all probability on the observed rating) and 1 (all probability on the opposite end of the scale). Over a test set, we average RPS over each artifact's human ratings, then over artifacts.

Intuition 1: five yes/no questions

At each threshold j the judge answers "is the rating at most j?" with a probability Qj, and the human rating gives the true answer. Each term is the squared error of one yes/no forecast (its Brier score), so RPS is the average squared error over five questions: "at most 0?", "at most 1?", up to "at most 4?". Read with the survival function, the questions become "does the rating exceed j?".

Intuition 2: the gap between two staircases

The judge's CDF is a staircase rising from 0 to 1. The observed rating is a single step at r. RPS adds up the squared vertical gaps between them at each threshold. A near miss leaves gaps at few thresholds, a far miss at many.

Intuition 3: distance in rating points

For integer ratings, 5 · RPS(q, r) = E|R − r| − ½ E|R − R′|, where R and R′ are two independent ratings drawn from the judge's forecast. The first term is the expected distance in rating points between a rating sampled from the judge and the human's rating. The second subtracts half the judge's own spread, so spreading probability is not penalized for its own sake. This is the discrete case of the continuous ranked probability score identity (Gneiting & Raftery 2007).

Why RPS is proper: it rewards the true distribution

If the human rating is drawn from a distribution p with CDF P, the expected RPS splits into two parts:

E[RPS] = (1/5) Σj ( Qj − Pj )² + (1/5) Σj Pj ( 1 − Pj )
= judge's error (≥ 0, zero only if q = p) + rater disagreement (same for every judge)

The second part is the noise floor set by how much raters disagree; no judge can beat it. The first part is zero only when the judge reports exactly the human rating distribution. So a judge gets its best expected score only by predicting the full distribution, including the disagreement. That property is called strict propriety.

Example that correlation cannot see. Raters split evenly between 3 and 5 on an artifact. Judge A predicts that split; Judge B puts all probability on 4. Both have expected rating 4, so exchanging these forecasts leaves a panel's mean-based correlation unchanged. If every expected rating is constant, correlation is undefined for both. Their expected RPS is not: 0.10 for Judge A (all of it rater disagreement) versus 0.20 for Judge B. A uniform forecast over 0–5 scores 0.206.

Why mean-based metrics miss this: the mean is the sum of the survival function. For ratings 0–5, the expected rating equals the sum of the survival probabilities over the five thresholds: E[R] = S0 + S1 + S2 + S3 + S4, where Sj = Pr[rating > j]. Mean squared error between expected ratings sees a forecast only through this sum. Correlation across artifacts sees even less, because it also ignores any shift or positive rescaling of the means. So survival gaps of opposite sign at different thresholds cancel in every mean-based metric; RPS squares each threshold's gap before adding, so they cannot. In the example, Judge A has S = (1, 1, 1, 0.5, 0.5) and Judge B has S = (1, 1, 1, 1, 0). Both sum to 4, so the gaps (0, 0, 0, +0.5, −0.5) cancel in the mean, while their squares give the excess expected RPS (0.25 + 0.25) / 5 = 0.10.

Why the cumulative form: robustness with few ratings

Each artifact has only two or three human ratings, so its rating histogram has at least three of six bins empty. Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(q ‖ p̂) is infinite if q assigns mass to an empty empirical bin. The reverse direction, KL(p̂ ‖ q), is finite when q is positive on observed ratings and differs from empirical cross-entropy only by a judge-independent entropy term. Empty empirical bins do not invalidate individual-rating cross-entropy or categorical Brier scores. Plug-in total variation distance (TVD) is biased upward even for the true distribution.

Lower is better. Both plug-in distances reward the overconfident forecast over the truth; only RPS puts the truth first. Even the true distribution's expected TVD to its own histogram is 0.48, 0.288 and 0.23 with 1, 2 and 3 ratings.

RPS avoids the improper plug-in comparison by scoring individual ratings. Each rating supplies a binary answer at every threshold, even when an empirical CDF is zero or one. With m independent ratings, the empirical CDF has variance Pj(1 − Pj)/m. Averaging individual-rating RPS gives the full disagreement floor Pj(1 − Pj) in expectation, independent of m. The floor cancels in expected differences between judges; observed paired differences remain noisy. Squaring the gaps makes the score proper, while the cumulative representation uses rating order.

Connection to the elusive paper. Schaeffer et al. (2024, arXiv 2406.04391) compared distributions of per-sample score–compute correlations through survival functions (complementary CDFs). Their stated reason was to read off what fraction of samples lies above any threshold. They summarized those distributions with the area under the survival function and with Wasserstein distances, which are also defined through CDFs. RPS applies the same cumulative view to scoring a forecast against observed ratings.

RPS as a distance between distributions, and "root RPS"

If the human distribution p were known, the judge's-error term above, (1/5) Σj (Qj − Pj)², would be a squared ℓ2 distance between the two CDFs. That is one fifth of ∫(Q − P)², the Cramér distance as defined by Bellemare et al. (2017), and on the 0–5 integer scale it equals one tenth of the energy distance (Székely & Rizzo 2013). Its square root is a true metric between rating distributions: a principled "root RPS".

In practice p is unknown, so we average RPS over observed ratings. The disagreement floor is the same for every judge, so differences in mean RPS between judges are unbiased estimates of differences in those squared CDF distances to the human distribution, divided by five. Here "root RPS" means the square root of the excess, not of the observed loss. Taking the square root of a mean RPS does not change how judges are ranked; taking it per rating before averaging would lose this unbiasedness. Bellemare et al. also show that the Cramér distance, unlike the Wasserstein distance, has unbiased sample gradients, so RPS also works as a training loss.

Why RPS is the right primary metric for LLM judges of human ratings

  • The ratings are ordered. Missing by one point should cost less than missing by four. RPS does this; cross-entropy and the Brier score do not.
  • It targets the whole rating distribution. Correlation ignores calibration and spread, and mean squared error only checks the mean. RPS is minimized only by the true distribution.
  • It works with two or three ratings per artifact. Each observed rating is scored directly through the CDF, so no per-artifact histogram has to be estimated, empty rating categories do not distort the score, and one rating per artifact already gives an unbiased estimate.
  • It is bounded and stable. RPS stays in [0, 1]. Cross-entropy can be dominated by a single rating that got almost no probability: in archived experiment 11, judge mixtures had higher held-out cross-entropy than a constant forecast (5.43 vs 1.85 nats, driven by a 10−6 probability floor), while their held-out RPS was lower (0.137 vs 0.183).
  • It is interpretable. Five times the normalized score has the absolute-error form in rating points, and the decomposition separates the judge's error from the rater-disagreement floor.

Limitations

RPS assumes equally spaced ratings (a miss from 0 to 1 costs the same as one from 4 to 5) and weights all thresholds equally. It is less sensitive than cross-entropy to the probability given to rare ratings, which is why cross-entropy stays as a secondary metric. With two or three ratings per artifact the disagreement floor is estimated noisily, so judges should be compared by paired differences on the same artifacts. Some references omit the 1/5 factor, giving values five times ours.

Galdran (2023, arXiv 2309.08701) notes two more behaviors. A confident miss is penalized only linearly with distance (a point mass d ratings away scores d/5). RPS can also prefer a symmetric forecast to one with more probability on the observed rating. That paper proposes a squared-absolute variant, but in our numeric check the variant is not proper. For raters spread (0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1) over 0–5, the concentrated forecast (0, 0, 0.9, 0.1, 0, 0) scores better on the variant (0.498) than the true distribution (0.680), while RPS prefers the truth (0.178 vs 0.246). So we keep RPS.
