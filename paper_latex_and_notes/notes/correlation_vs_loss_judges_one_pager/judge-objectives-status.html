Correlation vs loss for training LLM judges

Should we train or select an LLM judge by its correlation with human ratings, or by a proper loss against the human rating distribution on the 0–5 scale? Here is the evidence so far.

8-minute walkthrough: bottom line (1 min) → held-out human-rating plot (3 min) → synthetic figures (2 min) → does correlation win with little data? (1 min) → what RPS is → open items (1 min).

Experiments 00–02 and 06–08 are active; 11–14 are archived. Nothing is running. Unless a number is marked otherwise, it is measured on held-out test data that was not used to train or choose the judge.

Bottom line

  • In theory, loss wins clearly. In a synthetic population, correlation cannot tell the true rating distribution from wrong ones with the same mean, but cross-entropy picks the true one (exp 01).
  • On real human ratings, held-out test ranked probability score (RPS) is a tie. RPS measures how well a judge's predicted distribution over the 0–5 ratings matches each human rating; it ranges from 0 to 1 and lower is better (appendix). With the 5 frontier judges, test RPS of the correlation-trained judge minus test RPS of the loss-trained judge is +0.0056 [−0.0059, +0.0181] (exp 12). For scale, the loss-trained judge's test RPS is 0.136. On held-out data each judge wins on the kind of metric it was trained for: the loss-trained judge has lower test cross-entropy, the correlation-trained judge higher test Spearman.
  • Little data does not rescue correlation, with one exception. In synthetic tests (exp 08), correlation training beats loss training on held-out accuracy only when the target varies mainly in rater disagreement and its mean says little, and then only up to 96 ratings. Wherever the mean carries the signal, even linearly, loss training wins from 12 ratings (details).
  • The human data are the limit: 25 tasks, 75 artifacts and 195 expert ratings, all used in earlier studies. With a larger, noisier pool of judges, loss-trained mixtures have clearly lower test RPS (exp 14).

Setup and notation

②Correlation-trained judge: mixture weights over a fixed pool of base judges, or one base judge picked from the pool, chosen on training tasks to maximize Pearson or Spearman between the judge's expected rating and the human mean rating. ④Loss-trained judge: the same choice (mixture weights, or one base judge), made on training tasks to minimize cross-entropy against the human ratings. ③Choose by loss on a separate validation split. Not run yet.

Both kinds of judge are then scored with the same metrics on the same held-out test tasks. Method names describe how a judge was trained, never the metric it is scored with.

What "judge" and "training" mean here. A base judge is one model with one fixed prompt: the 5 frontier judges are GPT-6 Sol (high effort) and Claude Opus 5.5 (max effort) with prompts H0, H1 or H2, and exp 14 adds 39 open-weight model × prompt judges. Each base judge's three sampled 0–5 scores per artifact form its predicted rating distribution. "Training" only (a) sets the weights of a mixture over these fixed base judges' distributions, or (b) picks the single best base judge from the pool. Picking one base judge is a coarse choice among 5 or 44 model × prompt configurations. No experiment here searches over or rewrites prompts or harnesses, for example an agent iteratively improving the judge prompt against correlation versus against a proper loss. The closest is exp 12's fixed-split check, where Claude Code agents chose mixture weights from training data only.

RPS
Ranked probability score of the judge's predicted rating distribution against each human rating: (1/5) Σk=0..4 (Q(k) − 1{rating ≤ k})², averaged over ratings and artifacts. Range 0 to 1, lower is better. The primary metric. The appendix explains it with examples.
CE
Cross-entropy (log loss): average −ln q(rating), in nats, lower is better.
TVD
Total variation distance to each artifact's human rating histogram, 0 to 1, lower is better.
Intervals
95% bootstrap intervals over tasks (exps 11–14) or task families with refitting (exp 02). No p-values were computed.

Why RPS and the correlation objectives are not convex in W: each is a bounded function of W, and a bounded function on all of ℝd that is not constant cannot be convex. CE is unbounded and is convex here (it is multinomial logistic regression). Experiment 07 (Figure 7) logged convergence. CE, RPS, Kendall and the calibrated head converge in 97–100% of fits. Pearson-trained fits never converge within 400 iterations from 32 training tasks on, and 91% still hit a 2,000-iteration budget. Best-of-5 starts with a 5× budget improve their training objective, but every held-out test RPS crossover stays the same.

Notation: rating distributions and what each objective can learn

Bin(n, θ)
Binomial: n trials, success probability θ. Pr[R = r] = C(n, r) θr (1 − θ)n−r; mean nθ; variance nθ(1 − θ).
BetaBin(n, α, β)
Beta-binomial: draw θ ~ Beta(α, β), then R ~ Bin(n, θ). With α = μκ and β = (1 − μ)κ: mean nμ, variance nμ(1 − μ)(κ + n)/(κ + 1). κ sets rater disagreement; small κ splits raters toward 0 and 5.
Cat(p0, …, p5)
A general distribution over the six ratings: five free probabilities.
Data
An item's ratings r1, …, rm are independent draws, with counts nk = #{i : ri = k}.

Judge model in exp 13: qW(r | x) = softmax(W⊤φ(x))r, with φ(x) = 13 cosine and sine features of x and W a 13 × 6 matrix. Every objective fits this same family; only the objective differs.

General lesson. For a one-parameter family such as Bin(n, θ), the mean is a sufficient statistic, so a correct mean gives a correct distribution. Human ratings are not one-parameter. Disagreement (spread, raters split between 0 and 5) is extra information that only the full rating counts carry, and only proper losses use it. Via E[R] = Σj Sj, mean-based objectives compress those counts to one number per item.

Held-out test RPS on human ratings: correlation-trained vs loss-trained

Data
Each judge is trained on the human ratings of training tasks, then scored on held-out test tasks it never saw during training (exps 12, 14: 1,000 random splits of 20 training and 5 test tasks; exp 02: 5-fold task-family splits, repeated over 5 partitions). These 75 artifacts were used in earlier studies, so this is retrospective, not a fresh test set.
Plotted
Test RPS of the correlation-trained judge (②) minus test RPS of the loss-trained judge (④), both on the same test artifacts. Both numbers are the same metric, RPS, in the same units. "Correlation-trained" and "loss-trained" describe how each judge was built, not how it is scored: like two students who studied in different ways taking the same exam. Example, the "Exp 12 · 5 frontier judges" mixture row: judge ② has test RPS 0.1419 and judge ④ has 0.1362, so the difference is about +0.006 (reported +0.0056 before rounding).
Reading
+0.05 means the correlation-trained judge's test RPS is 0.05 higher (worse) than the loss-trained judge's; −0.05 means 0.05 lower (better). For scale, typical test RPS values are 0.14 to 0.33 (right-hand column), and predicting the training tasks' rating histogram for every artifact gives 0.184.

Synthetic results

Exp 01 · correlation cannot identify the distribution

Known binomial population, nine fixed candidate forecasts. Each criterion chooses a candidate on 48 validation items; the choice is scored exactly against the true distribution on 48 separate test items. Five candidates share the true mean, so Pearson, Spearman, Kendall and mean squared error tie them in all 256 replications.

Cross-entropy chooses the true model in 256/256 replications with 2–3 validation ratings per item and 249/256 with one. Test CE of the chosen candidate is 1.328 nats choosing by CE vs 1.995 choosing by Pearson with a random tie-break (tied range 1.328–3.348).

Exp 13 · learning a judge on a nonlinear toy

Built to illustrate the argument, so it is not evidence about real ratings. Judges are trained on n sampled training tasks and scored on 500 fresh test tasks against the true distribution. In the large-n limit, the Pearson-trained judge with a calibrated head has the best test correlations (0.97–0.99), yet its test RPS stays about 0.006–0.007 above the true distribution's. The CE-trained judge's gap keeps shrinking, to 0.0007–0.0037 at 2,048 training tasks.

Below about 30 training tasks (our real scale), the raw Pearson-trained judge's test RPS was as good or better (0.0097 vs 0.0113 above the truth at 20 tasks).

Exp 13 · setup (synthetic toy that works by construction)

Each synthetic task has one feature x. Human ratings on 0–5 follow a beta-binomial whose mean and disagreement both depend on x. Every judge is the same flexible model of x; only the objective used to fit it differs. Each judge is trained on n tasks with 3 ratings each, then scored on 500 fresh test tasks against the true rating distribution.

Tasks
x \sim \mathrm{Unif}[-1,1]
Human ratings
R \mid x \sim \mathrm{BetaBin}\big(5,\ \mu(x)\kappa(x),\ (1-\mu(x))\kappa(x)\big),\qquad \mathbb{E}[R\mid x]=5\mu(x),\qquad \mathrm{Var}[R\mid x]=5\mu(x)\big(1-\mu(x)\big)\frac{\kappa(x)+5}{\kappa(x)+1}
Disagreement
\kappa(x)=\exp\big(1.5+2.2\cos(1.5\pi x)\big)\in[0.50,\ 40.4]. Small κ splits raters toward 0 and 5; large κ means raters agree.
Scenario A: "same mean"
\mu_A(x)=0.5+0.08\,x \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[R\mid x]=2.5+0.4\,x\in[2.1,\ 2.9]. The mean moves at most 0.4 rating points from 2.5, while at μ = 0.5 the variance ranges from 1.37 (κ ≈ 40, raters agree) to 4.59 (κ ≈ 0.50, raters split). Almost all differences between tasks are in disagreement, not in the mean.
Scenario B
\mu_B(x)=0.1+0.8\left(\tfrac{x+1}{2}\right)^2 \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[R\mid x] rises monotonically from 0.5 to 4.5, and κ(x) varies as above.
Training data
n tasks x_1,\dots,x_n, each with 3 ratings; empirical histogram \hat p_i(r)=\tfrac13\,\#\{m: r_{im}=r\}, its CDF \hat P_i(j) and mean \hat m_i.
Judge family
q_W(r\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\big(W^\top\phi(x)\big)_r,\quad \phi(x)=\big(1,\cos\pi x,\sin\pi x,\dots,\cos 6\pi x,\sin 6\pi x\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{13},\quad W\in\mathbb{R}^{13\times 6},\quad m_W(x)=\textstyle\sum_r r\,q_W(r\mid x)
CE-trained (④)
\min_W\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{5}\hat p_i(r)\,\big[-\log q_W(r\mid x_i)\big]+\frac1n\|W\|^2
RPS-trained
\min_W\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac15\sum_{j=0}^{4}\big(Q_W(j\mid x_i)-\hat P_i(j)\big)^2+\frac1n\|W\|^2
Pearson-trained (②)
\max_W\ \operatorname{corr}_{i=1..n}\big(m_W(x_i),\ \hat m_i\big)-\frac1n\|W\|^2
Pearson constrains only the mean m_W(x), and only up to an increasing affine map. The rest of the distribution's shape is left to the starting point (the average training rating histogram) and the ridge penalty.
Pearson + calibrated head
A two-stage "score, then calibrate" judge. Stage 1: train W by Pearson as above, giving one score per task, s(x)=m_W(x). Stage 2: standardize it on the training tasks, z(x)=(s(x)-\bar s)/\mathrm{sd}(s), and fit a small six-way softmax on that single number,
q_{\text{head}}(r\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\big(a_r+b_r\,z(x)\big),\qquad (a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^6\times\mathbb{R}^6\ \text{fitted by CE on the training ratings.}
"Calibrated" means the head turns the score into probabilities for each rating that match observed rating frequencies. This mirrors the common practice of building a judge that outputs a scalar score and calibrating it afterward. Because the head sees only one number per task, it cannot recover disagreement that the score does not carry.
Test metrics
On 500 fresh tasks x'_t per seed, against the true distribution p: \text{RPS excess}=\frac1{500}\sum_t\frac15\sum_{j=0}^{4}\big(Q(j\mid x'_t)-P(j\mid x'_t)\big)^2 and \text{CE excess}=\frac1{500}\sum_t \mathrm{KL}\big(p(\cdot\mid x'_t)\,\|\,q(\cdot\mid x'_t)\big). Both are 0 for a perfect judge. The true distribution itself has test RPS 0.187 in scenario A and 0.136 in scenario B. 100 seeds per n.

Exp 13 · results: how many ratings until the loss-trained judge wins?

Why the number of ratings is the key quantity. The expensive part of building a judge is collecting the target labels it is trained and checked against: human ratings here. In formal verification the labels could instead be, for example, formal specification coverage (FSC) verdicts from an AI-guided prover. So every figure that varies the amount of training data marks the n, in tasks and in ratings, from which the loss-trained judge is better.

What the two rows of Figure 3 test. Neither row is the "linear" or "first-moment" case where correlation should do best.

  • Top row (scenario A): the mean barely changes (2.1–2.9) while disagreement changes about 80-fold. Almost all of the signal is in the second moment, which correlation cannot see. This tests whether there is information beyond the mean.
  • Bottom row (scenario B): the mean is informative and a nonlinear function of x, and disagreement also varies, which is closer to real human judgments. With enough data, the Pearson-trained judge learns the ordering: test Spearman against the true mean rises from 0.78 at 64 tasks to 0.97 at 2,048. It still loses on distribution metrics, because correlation fixes neither spread nor calibration.

Exp 06 · does training on a rank correlation, or a Pearson + Spearman mix, fix it?

Complete: 11 training sizes (4 to 2,048 tasks) × 100 seeds × 2 scenarios.

Same judge family, same seeds and the identical training and test tasks as exp 13. Three new training objectives:

  • Spearman-trained: maximizes Spearman between the judge's expected ratings and the training-task mean human ratings.
  • Kendall-trained: maximizes Kendall's tau between the same two quantities.
  • ½ Pearson + ½ Spearman-trained: maximizes the average of the two correlations.

Rank correlations are not differentiable, so training uses standard smooth versions (temperature 0.1 rating points). Refitting CE on the regenerated data reproduces exp 13's saved numbers to 1e-14.

No correlation objective fixes it.

  • Scenario B: the CE-trained judge beats all four correlation-trained judges from 4 tasks (12 ratings).
  • Scenario A: it beats Kendall-trained from 8 tasks (24 ratings) and Pearson-, Spearman- and ½ Pearson + ½ Spearman-trained from 64 tasks (192 ratings), on test RPS.
  • Size of the gap at 2,048 tasks: the correlation-trained judges' test RPS excess is about 9–26× the CE-trained judge's in scenario B (0.033–0.095 vs 0.0037) and about 35–45× in scenario A (0.025–0.031 vs 0.0007).
  • The mixed objective behaves like Pearson alone, so the weights barely matter. Kendall is the worst objective in scenario A.

Even on correlation, training on correlation is not better until the data are plentiful.

  • Scenario A: the CE-trained judge has the highest test correlation at every n, significantly higher than every correlation-trained judge from 4–16 tasks (12–48 ratings) on. At 2,048 tasks its test Pearson is 0.89, against 0.75–0.85 for the correlation-trained judges.
  • Scenario B, small n: the CE-trained judge leads by a wide margin. At 16 tasks (48 ratings) its test Pearson is 0.69, against 0.09–0.12 for the Pearson-, Spearman- and mixed-trained judges.
  • Scenario B, large n: the correlation-trained judges overtake on their own metric only from 256 tasks (768 ratings; Spearman- and Kendall-trained, on test Spearman) or 512 tasks (1,536 ratings; Pearson- and ½ Pearson + ½ Spearman-trained, on test Pearson). At 2,048 tasks the Spearman-trained judge's test Spearman is 0.982 vs 0.948, yet its test RPS excess is about 17× the CE-trained judge's (0.062 vs 0.0037).

A likely reason for the small-n gap: the CE-trained judge learns from every rating (the full histogram per task), while the correlation-trained judges see one noisy mean per task.

Exp 07 · do these results depend on the optimizer?

Experiments 13 and 06 fit each judge once, from one start, with at most 400 iterations, and the correlation objectives are not convex. Experiment 07 refits every objective on the same tasks (30 seeds, 4 to 1,024 training tasks) three ways:

  • the original setting;
  • the original start with a 5× budget (2,000 iterations);
  • the best of 5 starts with that budget, chosen by the training objective (test data never used).

Does correlation win with little data? (exp 08)

The conjecture (Brando, 11-13-2026). With little rating data, training a judge on correlation might work better, because it only has to get the first moment (the mean) right. Cross-entropy tries to fit structure the data cannot yet support. If so, the number of ratings a loss-trained judge needs before it wins should grow with the complexity of the target rating distribution.

Verdict against the rule fixed before measurement: neither hypothesis holds as stated.

  • Correlation's low-data edge appears only in scenario A (up to 96 ratings), not in C or D, where the conjecture predicted it most clearly. The ratings needed for CE to win are the same 12 in B, C and D.
  • On held-out correlation, the correlation-trained judges overtake only with plenty of data: from 512 tasks (1,536 ratings) in B and C, and 1,024 (3,072) in D; never in A.
  • Validation tuning is noisy with few tasks. It moves scenario A's crossover from 64 to 32 tasks, but tuned CE trails fixed-ridge Pearson at 4–16 tasks in A and at 4 tasks in D.
  • The Pearson-trained judge (②) ends up worse than a constant forecast on test RPS at large n in every scenario: from 128 tasks in A, 8 in B, 16 in C and 256 in D. Its test correlation meanwhile reaches 0.85–0.99.

What this says about the conjecture. The part that survives: when the target's structure lies in higher moments that too few ratings cannot pin down, and the mean carries little signal, matching the mean while keeping the average spread is the best a learner can do early. Correlation training does exactly that (scenario A). The part that fails: wherever the mean carries information, even linearly, the loss-trained judge learns it at least as fast as the correlation-trained one. So the ratings needed depend on where the target's information lies, not on its complexity alone; scenario B is as complex as A but crosses at 12 ratings. And whatever the data size, correlation stays the wrong primary evaluation metric (next section). Which scenario real human ratings resemble is an open question, and it decides whether correlation training could ever help at small n.

Why test correlation is the wrong primary metric (for reviewer 2)

  1. Even on correlation, training on correlation buys little. With realistic numbers of ratings, the loss-trained judge has equal or higher held-out test correlation (Figure 6). Correlation-trained judges overtake only with many ratings, and then only slightly, while their rating distributions are far worse (Figure 5).
  2. Correlation cannot see what matters. Maximizing Pearson is the same as minimizing squared error between z-scored judge and human ratings. So Pearson is unchanged by any shift or positive rescaling of the predicted ratings, exactly the bias and scale terms of MSE (derivation), and Spearman by any increasing transform. Both see one number per item, the mean, via E[R] = Σj Sj. So neither detects miscalibration, bias or rater disagreement. Figure 2: correlation ties five different distributions in every replication. Exp 00: a judge that only ever outputs 4 or 5 still has Pearson 0.877, against 0.900 for a faithful judge.
  3. Correlation is not a proper scoring rule. It does not reward reporting the true rating distribution. Its value also depends on how spread out the test items are, not just on the judge, so it cannot be averaged item by item or compared across test sets.
  4. What correlation is still good for. If the only use is ranking items, Spearman is a reasonable secondary diagnostic. To choose or train a judge that must reproduce human ratings, use a proper loss such as RPS, and report correlations alongside it.

Limits to keep in mind

  • All human-rating experiments reuse one small rating set: 25 Python tasks, 19 task families, 2–3 expert ratings per artifact. Repeated splits and extra judges do not add independent human evidence.
  • "Held out" means held out from that fit. The ratings were used in earlier judge selection, so every human result is retrospective. Exps 11, 12 and 14 split by task, not task family, so related tasks can land on both sides.
  • The judges are mixtures of scalar scores, not native probability outputs. Their predicted rating distributions are poorly calibrated: a constant forecast has lower test CE.
  • No p-values: no confirmatory test was declared in advance. Verdicts above use whether the 95% interval excludes 0.

Appendix: Pearson correlation written as a squared loss

Let X be the judge's expected rating on a random test artifact, X=\mathbb{E}_{q}[R], and Y the human mean rating on the same artifact.

A. Population form

\rho_{X,Y}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\,\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}

If X and Y are centered (\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]=0), this simplifies to \rho_{X,Y}=\mathbb{E}[XY]/\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}.

B. Sample form over N held-out test artifacts

Stack the judge's expected ratings and the human mean ratings into vectors \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^N, center them, and normalize:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}-\bar{x}\,\mathbf{1},\quad \tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{y}-\bar{y}\,\mathbf{1},\qquad r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2}=\cos\theta=\hat{\mathbf{x}}^\top\hat{\mathbf{y}},\quad \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|_2},\ \hat{\mathbf{y}}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_2}

Pearson correlation is the cosine of the angle between the centered vectors.

C. Pearson is a squared loss on standardized scores

With z-scores Z_X=(X-\mu_X)/\sigma_X and Z_Y=(Y-\mu_Y)/\sigma_Y:

\mathbb{E}\big[(Z_X-Z_Y)^2\big]=2\,(1-\rho_{X,Y}),\qquad \|\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2=2\,(1-r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})

So maximizing Pearson is the same as minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between z-scored judge ratings and z-scored human ratings. Using (1-\rho)^2 instead gives the same optimum, because it is a decreasing function of ρ.

D. What plain MSE keeps and Pearson throws away

With population standard deviations s_x=\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2} and likewise s_y, the MSE between raw ratings splits exactly into three parts:

\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-y_i)^2=\underbrace{(\bar{x}-\bar{y})^2}_{\text{bias}}+\underbrace{(s_x-s_y)^2}_{\text{scale mismatch}}+\underbrace{2\,s_x s_y\,(1-r_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}})}_{\text{the only part Pearson sees}}

Pearson drops the bias and scale terms: any judge \mathbf{x}'=a\,\mathbf{x}+b\,\mathbf{1} with a>0 has exactly the same r. Example: a judge that predicts x=0.1+0.08\,y, always between 0.1 and 0.5 on the 0–5 scale, has r = 1.000 but MSE = 6.68 = bias² 4.84 + scale mismatch² 1.84 + 0.

E. Both see only the mean

Each entry x_i=\mathbb{E}_{q_i}[R]=\sum_{j=0}^{4}S_{ij} compresses the judge's whole distribution over the six ratings into one number, and y_i does the same to the humans' ratings. So even plain MSE identifies only the conditional mean, and Pearson sees less than that. Distributions that share every mean get identical Pearson and MSE (Figure 2). RPS and cross-entropy compare the whole distribution on every artifact.

Every identity above was checked numerically on 1,000 random cases (maximum error 9e-15).

Appendix: the ranked probability score (RPS)

Ranked probability score (RPS) is a standard scoring rule in weather forecasting, introduced by Epstein (1969), but it is uncommon in machine learning. It scores a predicted probability distribution over ordered categories, such as our 0–5 ratings, against the rating a human actually gave.

Definition

For one artifact, the judge outputs probabilities q0, …, q5 over the six ratings. Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is Qj = Pr[rating ≤ j] = q0 + … + qj, and its survival function is Sj = Pr[rating > j] = 1 − Qj. For an observed human rating r:

RPS(q, r) = (1/5) · Σj=0..4 ( Qj − 1{r ≤ j} )² = (1/5) · Σj=0..4 ( Sj − 1{r > j} )²

Threshold j = 5 is skipped because Q5 = 1 for every forecast. Dividing by the five thresholds puts RPS between 0 (all probability on the observed rating) and 1 (all probability on the opposite end of the scale). Over a test set, we average RPS over each artifact's human ratings, then over artifacts.

Intuition 1: five yes/no questions

At each threshold j the judge answers "is the rating at most j?" with a probability Qj, and the human rating gives the true answer. Each term is the squared error of one yes/no forecast (its Brier score), so RPS is the average squared error over five questions: "at most 0?", "at most 1?", up to "at most 4?". Read with the survival function, the questions become "does the rating exceed j?".

Intuition 2: the gap between two staircases

The judge's CDF is a staircase rising from 0 to 1. The observed rating is a single step at r. RPS adds up the squared vertical gaps between them at each threshold. A near miss leaves gaps at few thresholds, a far miss at many.

Figure A2. Example: a human rating r = 4 and a judge forecast q = (0, 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.4, 0.2) over ratings 0–5. Squared gaps 0 + 0 + 0.01 + 0.16 + 0.04 = 0.21, so RPS = 0.21 / 5 = 0.042.

Lower is better in every column. Cross-entropy is infinite (or set by a probability floor) whenever the observed rating got zero probability, and the Brier score treats ratings as unordered labels. Only RPS scores the near miss as better than the far miss.

Intuition 3: distance in rating points

For integer ratings, 5 · RPS(q, r) = E|R − r| − ½ E|R − R′|, where R and R′ are two independent ratings drawn from the judge's forecast. The first term is the expected distance in rating points between a rating sampled from the judge and the human's rating. The second subtracts half the judge's own spread, so spreading probability is not penalized for its own sake. This is the discrete case of the continuous ranked probability score identity (Gneiting & Raftery 2007).

Why RPS is proper: it rewards the true distribution

If the human rating is drawn from a distribution p with CDF P, the expected RPS splits into two parts:

E[RPS] = (1/5) Σj ( Qj − Pj )² + (1/5) Σj Pj ( 1 − Pj )
= judge's error (≥ 0, zero only if q = p) + rater disagreement (same for every judge)

The second part is the noise floor set by how much raters disagree; no judge can beat it. The first part is zero only when the judge reports exactly the human rating distribution. So a judge gets its best expected score only by predicting the full distribution, including the disagreement. That property is called strict propriety.

Example that correlation cannot see. Raters split evenly between 3 and 5 on an artifact. Judge A predicts that split; Judge B puts all probability on 4. Both have expected rating 4, so their correlation with human mean ratings is identical. Their expected RPS is not: 0.10 for Judge A (all of it rater disagreement) versus 0.20 for Judge B. A uniform forecast over 0–5 scores 0.206.

Why mean-based metrics miss this: the mean is the sum of the survival function. For ratings 0–5, the expected rating equals the sum of the survival probabilities over the five thresholds: E[R] = S0 + S1 + S2 + S3 + S4, where Sj = Pr[rating > j]. Mean squared error between expected ratings sees a forecast only through this sum. Correlation across artifacts sees even less, because it also ignores any shift or rescaling of the means. So survival gaps of opposite sign at different thresholds cancel in every mean-based metric; RPS squares each threshold's gap before adding, so they cannot. In the example, Judge A has S = (1, 1, 1, 0.5, 0.5) and Judge B has S = (1, 1, 1, 1, 0). Both sum to 4, so the gaps (0, 0, 0, +0.5, −0.5) cancel in the mean, while their squares give the excess expected RPS (0.25 + 0.25) / 5 = 0.10.

Why the cumulative form: robustness with few ratings

Each artifact has only two or three human ratings, so its rating histogram has at least three of six bins empty. Distances computed bin by bin against such a histogram are dominated by the empty bins. Kullback–Leibler divergence becomes infinite or is set by a probability floor, and total variation distance (TVD) is biased upward even for the true distribution.

Lower is better. Both plug-in distances reward the overconfident forecast over the truth; only RPS puts the truth first. Even the true distribution's expected TVD to its own histogram is 0.48, 0.288 and 0.23 with 1, 2 and 3 ratings.

The cumulative form avoids this. Each Qj = Pr[rating ≤ j] pools every rating category at or below j, so every rating informs every threshold and no threshold is ever empty. The empirical CDF at a threshold is an average of 0/1 indicators, so it is an unbiased estimate. Its sampling noise is exactly the rater-disagreement term, which is the same for every judge and cancels when two judges are compared on the same artifacts. Squaring the gaps, rather than taking their absolute values (the Wasserstein-1 column), is what makes the score proper.

Connection to the elusive paper. Schaeffer et al. (2024, arXiv 2406.04391) compared distributions of per-sample score–compute correlations through survival functions (complementary CDFs). Their stated reason was to read off what fraction of samples lies above any threshold. They summarized those distributions with the area under the survival function and with Wasserstein distances, which are also defined through CDFs. RPS applies the same cumulative view to scoring a forecast against observed ratings.

RPS as a distance between distributions, and "root RPS"

If the human distribution p were known, the judge's-error term above, (1/5) Σj (Qj − Pj)², would be a squared ℓ2 distance between the two CDFs. That is the squared Cramér distance (Bellemare et al. 2017), and on the 0–5 integer scale it equals one tenth of the energy distance (Székely & Rizzo 2013). Its square root is a true metric between rating distributions: a principled "root RPS".

In practice p is unknown, so we average RPS over observed ratings. The disagreement floor is the same for every judge, so differences in mean RPS between judges are unbiased estimates of differences in their squared Cramér distances to the human distribution. We found no standard metric called "root RPS". Taking the square root of a mean RPS does not change how judges are ranked; taking it per rating before averaging would lose this unbiasedness. Bellemare et al. also show that the Cramér distance, unlike the Wasserstein distance, has unbiased sample gradients, so RPS also works as a training loss.

Why RPS is the right primary metric for LLM judges of human ratings

  • The ratings are ordered. Missing by one point should cost less than missing by four. RPS does this; cross-entropy and the Brier score do not.
  • It targets the whole rating distribution. Correlation ignores calibration and spread, and mean squared error only checks the mean. RPS is minimized only by the true distribution.
  • It works with two or three ratings per artifact. Each observed rating is scored directly through the CDF, so no per-artifact histogram has to be estimated, empty rating categories do not distort the score, and one rating per artifact already gives an unbiased estimate.
  • It is bounded and stable. RPS stays in [0, 1]. Cross-entropy can be dominated by a single rating that got almost no probability: in archived experiment 11, judge mixtures had higher held-out cross-entropy than a constant forecast (5.43 vs 1.85 nats, driven by a 10−6 probability floor), while their held-out RPS was lower (0.137 vs 0.183).
  • It is interpretable. It reads in rating points, and the decomposition separates the judge's error from the rater-disagreement floor.

Limitations

RPS assumes equally spaced ratings (a miss from 0 to 1 costs the same as one from 4 to 5) and weights all thresholds equally. It is less sensitive than cross-entropy to the probability given to rare ratings, which is why cross-entropy stays as a secondary metric. With two or three ratings per artifact the disagreement floor is estimated noisily, so judges should be compared by paired differences on the same artifacts. Some references omit the 1/5 factor, giving values five times ours.

Galdran (2023, arXiv 2309.08701) notes two more behaviors. A confident miss is penalized only linearly with distance (a point mass d ratings away scores d/5). RPS can also prefer a symmetric forecast to one with more probability on the observed rating. That paper proposes a squared-absolute variant, but in our numeric check the variant is not proper. For raters spread (0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1, 0.1) over 0–5, the concentrated forecast (0, 0, 0.9, 0.1, 0, 0) scores better on the variant (0.498) than the true distribution (0.680), while RPS prefers the truth (0.178 vs 0.246). So we keep RPS.
