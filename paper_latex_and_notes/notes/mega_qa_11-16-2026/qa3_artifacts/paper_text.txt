Correlation Alone Does Not Identify a Judge’s
Conditional Rating Distribution
Anonymous submission
Abstract
A judge can predict the correct mean rating while assigning the
wrong conditional distribution to every input. Correlation be-
tween scalar judge and human scores cannot distinguish such
forecasts. We argue for evaluating the conditional rating dis-
tribution with proper probability losses, alongside scalar asso-
ciation and agreement diagnostics. A binomial construction
gives five candidate distributions with identical conditional
means and distinct expected cross-entropy, Brier score and
ranked probability score. With one synthetic rating per item,
cross-entropy selects the true distribution in 249 of 256 repli-
cations. Pearson, Spearman, Kendall and scalar mean squared
error retain all five candidates as ties throughout. Training a
flexible judge on synthetic ratings, cross-entropy beats cor-
relation training on held-out rating distributions from 12 rat-
ings in three targets with informative means; in the fourth
target, correlation training helps with few ratings when vari-
ation lies mainly in rater disagreement. We enumerate these
ties, reproduce the older marginal-histogram example, and re-
tain inconclusive historical theorem-judge evidence. The re-
sult supports distribution-aware evaluation without implying
human generalization, universal finite-sample superiority, or
that psychometric methods are unnecessary.
Introduction
A scalar agreement statistic does not determine whether a
judge predicts how human ratings vary across inputs. G-
Eval evaluates text judges using correlation with human
judgments (Liu et al. 2023). MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena
also study agreement with human preferences and document
judge biases (Zheng et al. 2023). These comparisons answer
useful questions, but do not by themselves identify a condi-
tional rating distribution.
We take the distribution as the target when the evalua-
tion requires probabilities for each possible rating. Proper
scoring rules provide an established framework for this tar-
get (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). Our contributions are a
controlled identification study that makes the information
discarded by scalar criteria explicit, and a synthetic learning
study that measures how many ratings proper-loss training

1. We construct conditional distributions that share their
means but differ under proper probability losses.
2. We evaluate selection using one, two and three synthetic
ratings per item, reporting every tied minimizer and com-
mon test metric.
3. We train a flexible judge by proper losses and Pearson
correlation on four synthetic targets, additionally testing
smooth Spearman and Kendall objectives on two targets,
and measure the tested sizes from which proper-loss train-
ing is better on held-out data.
4. We preserve historical null findings and identify the limits
of transferring this synthetic result to human-rated judges.

proper-loss training needs before it beats correlation training
on held-out data.

Interpretation.
Among these four targets, fixed-ridge
Pearson training helps at small sample sizes only in A,
where the mean carries little signal. The results are consis-
tent with regularization helping when few ratings constrain
the distribution poorly; they do not identify the best possible
small-sample strategy. In B, C and D, fixed-ridge CE training
achieves lower test RPS from the smallest tested size. The
contrast between A and B suggests that where the informa-
tion lies matters, beyond varying concentration alone. These
results use one input feature and one judge family; which
regime human ratings occupy is open.

Scope and Limitations
The construction deliberately includes the oracle and changes
distribution shape without changing the mean. It demon-
strates nonidentification by scalar summaries, not the preva-
lence of this failure among natural judges. The family split
holds out synthetic parameter bands; it is not evidence of
transfer across real tasks, solvers, raters or model families.
Independent binomial ratings omit rater heterogeneity and
dependence. The study trains no language model and uses
no new provider calls.
One observed rating can score a conditional forecast, but
learning a flexible conditional distribution requires data and
shared structure. In our learning study that requirement was
small when the target’s mean was informative (12 ratings) and
larger when its information lay mainly in rater disagreement
(192 ratings). The learning study uses one input feature, three
ratings per task and one judge family, and its correlation-
trained results reflect a non-convex optimizer, although the
tested restarts and larger budget did not change the RPS
crossovers. Neither a high correlation nor low finite-sample
proper loss establishes that an entire human population has
been learned. When only a conditional mean or ranking is
required, scalar objectives may be appropriate for that nar-
rower target. When the distribution is required, report proper
probability losses and keep association, absolute error and
bias as complementary diagnostics.
Conclusion
Correlation alone does not identify a judge’s conditional rat-
ing distribution. Proper probability losses identify that tar-
get in the population under their assumptions; sparse data
and optimization still determine empirical selection quality.
In synthetic learning experiments, proper-loss training beat
correlation training on held-out distributions from 12 rat-
ings in the three tested targets with informative means. Our
controlled studies support this distinction while preserving
inconclusive historical evidence.

Example: disagreement that correlation cannot see.

Why the cumulative form: robustness with few ratings.
Each artifact has only two or three human ratings, so its
rating histogram leaves at least three of the six bins empty.
For a forecast assigning positive mass to an unobserved cat-
egory, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence DKL(q∥ˆp) is
infinite unless the empirical histogram is smoothed. The re-

observed ratings and differs from empirical CE only by a
judge-independent entropy term. Empty empirical bins there-
fore do not invalidate individual-rating CE or the categorical
Brier score. Total variation distance (TVD) is biased upward
even for the true distribution. Suppose raters choose 3 with
probability 0.6 and 5 with probability 0.4. The true distri-
bution’s expected TVD to its own histogram of 1, 2 or 3
ratings is 0.48, 0.288 and 0.23. With one rating per artifact,
a forecast that puts all its mass on 3 scores better (0.40) than
the truth (0.48), so plug-in TVD rewards overconfidence.
Scoring each rating by the absolute gap between CDFs, the
one-rating Wasserstein-1 distance, has the same flaw (0.80
for the point mass vs 0.96 for the truth) because it is min-
imized by a point mass at the median. RPS ranks the truth
first (0.096 vs 0.160).

Why RPS suits judges of ordinal human ratings.
• Order matters. Ratings are ordered; RPS charges more
for larger misses. CE and the categorical Brier score treat
ratings as unordered labels.
• It targets the whole distribution. Being strictly proper,
RPS is minimized only by the true conditional rating
distribution. Correlation ignores calibration and spread,
and mean squared error identifies only the conditional
mean.
• It works with sparse ratings. Each observed rating is
scored directly through the CDF, so artifacts with one to
three ratings contribute without estimating a histogram,
and empty rating categories do not distort the score.
• It is bounded. RPS lies in [0, 1] and cannot be dominated
by one rating that received near-zero probability. CE can:
in our retrospective human-rating analysis (archived ex-
periment 11), judge mixtures had higher held-out CE than
a constant forecast of the training ratings (5.43 vs 1.85
nats), driven by the 10−6 probability floor, while their
held-out RPS was lower (0.137 vs 0.183).
• It is interpretable. The absolute-error form is in rating
points, and Eq. 13 separates the judge’s error from the
noise floor set by rater disagreement.

Historical judges.
We reuse the historical test split of the
section on historical theorem-judge evidence: 30 artifacts,
10 tasks and 78 expert ratings, all with prior exposure. For
each of the five pre-existing cached judges (none fitted here)
we compute the test–retest Eρ2 of one call from its three


repeated scores, and the held-out RPS of its three-call his-

judge (Eρ2 = 0.983) had the highest RPS (0.273), and the
judge with the lowest RPS (0.191) had Eρ2 = 0.818. The
held-out RPS of the most repeatable judge minus that of the
lowest-RPS judge was +0.082 [0.004, 0.168], a 95% per-

means the more repeatable judge was worse. Both judges
were identified on this same historical test split and then held
fixed in the bootstrap, as were raters and calls; the interval
is descriptive and does not adjust for selecting the pair. No
judge’s RPS was below that of a constant forecast equal to
the training-split rating histogram (0.180). All five judges had
higher point estimates of repeatability than the two experts’
inter-rater agreement (Eρ2 = 0.800 for one rating).
What generalizability theory does well.
Its intended use
is planning measurement: choosing how many raters and
repeated occasions to average. We simulated a crossed ar-
tifact × rater × occasion Gaussian model in two configu-
rations with equal total variance (3.0) but opposite optimal
designs (8 × 2 versus 2 × 8). In each of 256 replications, a
64 × 8 × 8 calibration study estimated the variance compo-
nents by constrained restricted maximum likelihood (which
matched closed-form analysis of variance in every replication
because no raw estimate was negative), chose the design with
the smallest estimated v∆among the three 16-rating alloca-
tions (8 × 2, 4 × 4, 2 × 8), and scored every design on 256
fresh artifacts with fresh raters and occasions. The chosen
design was the true optimal design in all 256 replications of
both configurations. Its held-out mean squared error around
the artifact’s universe score (expectation v∆) minus that of
the equal 4 × 4 allocation was −0.107 [−0.149, −0.065]
and −0.072 [−0.108, −0.035] (true difference −0.100, in
units where vu = 1; negative means lower error for the cho-
sen design; 95% Student-t intervals over 256 replications).
One of 36 in-model error checks missed the prespecified
3.5 Monte Carlo standard-error tolerance; a larger post-hoc
rerun was consistent with the formula and did not replace
the failed check. Separate tests examined dependence, task-
group heterogeneity and deployment shifts, limitations dis-
cussed by the source (Shavelson, Webb, and Rowley 1989).
With fixed designs, the independence-based prediction un-
derstated deployment error for correlated raters, heteroge-
neous task groups hid subgroup Φ differences (0.808 versus
0.748) behind one pooled Φ (0.777), and dependent calibra-
tion occasions inflated predicted Φ above realized deploy-
ment Φ (0.777) by +0.089 [0.085, 0.093] (predicted minus
realized). Under deployment shifts, narrowing artifact vari-
ance (vu: 1 →0.25) left the optimal design (8 × 2) and
squared error unchanged while lowering Φ from a predicted
0.775 to 0.465, whereas increasing artifact-by-occasion in-
stability (vub: 0.1 →1.2) reversed the optimal design from
the chosen 8 × 2 to 4 × 4 (held-out squared error of 4 × 4
minus 8 × 2: −0.147 [−0.198, −0.097]). We therefore use
proper probability losses to choose a judge and variance
components to plan rating effort; neither replaces the other.
