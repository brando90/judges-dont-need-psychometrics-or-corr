## OpenReview Submission Packet

- **Portal URL:** <https://openreview.net/group?id=AAAI.org/2027/Workshop/EvalAIScientists> (deadline `11-20-2026 06:00 UTC` = `11-19-2026 10:00 PM PST`).
- **Title:** `Correlation Alone Does Not Identify a Judge's Conditional Rating Distribution`
- **Track:** Position paper (`2–4` main-body pages; references excluded per workshop call).
- **TL;DR (228 chars):** `Correlation alone cannot identify a judge's conditional rating distribution; proper probability losses (CE, RPS) distinguish identical-mean forecasts and improve held-out RPS across synthetic and retrospective LLM-judge studies.`
- **Keywords:** `LLM-as-a-judge`, `proper scoring rules`, `ranked probability score`, `cross-entropy`, `calibration`, `evaluation methodology`
- **Abstract (single paragraph, matches `1-abstract.tex`):**
  > A judge can predict the correct mean rating while assigning the wrong conditional distribution to every input. Correlation between scalar judge and human scores cannot distinguish such forecasts. We argue for evaluating the conditional rating distribution with proper probability losses, alongside scalar association and agreement diagnostics. A binomial construction gives five candidate distributions with identical conditional means and distinct expected cross-entropy, Brier score and ranked probability score (RPS). With one synthetic rating per item, cross-entropy selects the true distribution in 249 of 256 replications. Pearson, Spearman, Kendall and scalar mean squared error retain all five candidates as ties throughout. Training a flexible judge on synthetic ratings, cross-entropy beats correlation training on held-out rating distributions from 12 ratings in three targets with informative means; in the fourth target, correlation training helps with few ratings when variation lies mainly in rater disagreement. We enumerate these ties, preserve inconclusive three-call historical theorem-judge evidence under empty-bin floors, and evaluate native six-category probability, prompt, harness, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) judges across ten open-weight and seven frontier models on the same retrospective human ratings: proper-loss mixtures, calibration heads, and LoRA weights improve held-out RPS over correlation fits, while coarse discrete single-prompt selection stays noisy. The result supports distribution-aware evaluation without implying zero-exposure human generalization, universal finite-sample superiority, or that psychometric methods are unnecessary.
