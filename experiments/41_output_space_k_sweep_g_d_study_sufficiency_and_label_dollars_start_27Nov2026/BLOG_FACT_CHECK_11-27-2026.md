## Answer

Brando asked: "so I guess this is correct?" Yes, mostly. Every number in the post reproduces from the saved results, and the mathematics of the appendix holds. What needs work is of three kinds.

1. Some sentences leave out a denominator or a caveat that the memo or the experiment itself states.
2. One caveat matters more than the others: a correlation-trained head followed by a calibration step closes most of the gap to the cross-entropy-trained head (Experiment 28 on DICES, Experiment 41 on seven more sets), and the post does not say so.
3. The page breaks four of the rules in the website repo's own CLAUDE.md: it has an appendix, a hidden Gemini link in the page source, a closing P.S., and no Acknowledgments section.

## 2. What needs one more clause (proposed text, nothing applied)

1. **The scaling-law sentence leaves out three caveats.** Three of the nine cross-entropy "fits" have only four budgets and one degree of freedom, and the saved table labels them indicative (B4); counting only fits with more than four budgets gives six of eleven (B5). The one Pearson "fit" has an exponent of at most 0.2, so it is a near-flat curve (B7), and in all eleven panels the Pearson-trained curve is flat, non-monotone, poorly fit or such a near-flat fit (B7b). The memo's own sentence states that the medians come from 8 of 25 cells for RPS and 20 of 25 for cross-entropy; the post drops both denominators (B9b). Proposed text: "The law fits the cross-entropy-trained curve in 9 of 11 panels (three of the nine have only four budgets, which the memo flags as indicative), with median exponents of 0.66 on ranked probability score (8 of 25 frozen-judge cells have an identified floor) and 0.82 on cross-entropy (20 of 25). It fits the correlation-trained curve in 1 of 11, and that curve is nearly flat, with exponent at most 0.2."
2. **"Five public rating sets and two model families" miscounts the eleven panels.** They are five panels from frozen frontier judges (a mean over five judges) plus six LoRA panels, four with Qwen3-4B and two with Llama-3.1-8B, on four public sets, one of which, HelpSteer2, is not among the five (B13b). Proposed text: "The memo fits a saturating power law to eleven learning curves of our own: five public rating sets scored by frozen frontier judges with a small calibration head, and six fine-tuned open-weight judges (two model families) on four public sets."
3. **The "4 to 8 ratings" sentence leaves out the caveat in the same sentence of Experiment 28.** Its README says the Pearson fit followed by a calibration step is better than the cross-entropy fit on DICES from 64 ratings (B13). Proposed addition after the claim: "(A correlation-trained head followed by a calibration step catches up on some sets; on DICES it is ahead of the cross-entropy fit from 64 ratings.)"
4. **"The code, data and every number in it are in the working repo" is too strong.** The memo's own corrections note (`paper_latex_and_notes/ICML_format_tech_memo_11-25-2026/QA_CORRECTIONS_11-26-2026.md`, items g7-M6 and g7-M7) records that some per-seed outputs and prepared rating bundles are not in the checkout. Proposed text: "The code and the saved results are in the working repo."
5. **Appendix A.1, "that second line is the Brier score ... scalar L2 and Brier are the same number."** This holds when the judge's score X is its probability of the positive class (the mean of its distribution). The appendix defines X as a sampled draw; for a sampled 0/1 draw E[(X − Y)²] = Brier(q) + q(1 − q), and at q = p = 1/2 it is 1/2 where the Brier score is 1/4 (B14, B14b). Also, with the sum over both classes that the appendix defines in D_B, the binary Brier score is twice the scalar squared error (B15). Proposed text: "On a binary label Y ∈ {0,1}, take X to be the judge's probability of 1, the mean of its distribution. Then the second line is the Brier score, and with the one-class convention scalar squared error and Brier are the same number (with the sum over both classes, Brier is twice it)."
6. **Appendix A.4, "D_B(p̂_test ‖ q̂) sits above D_B(p* ‖ q̂) by (1/k_c)(1 − ‖p*‖²)."** It is true in expectation over the resampled ratings (exact for k = 1 to 5, B16), not for every sample. Proposed text: "sits above ... in expectation by".
7. **"Both to evaluate judges and, increasingly, to tune them."** The repository's literature check (about 90 papers read by abstract and key passages, none end to end; see this repo's CLAUDE.md, section "How we choose (learn) a judge") found agreement with humans used to evaluate judges, found no paper that states maximizing correlation as its principle for choosing a judge, and found two papers reporting that correlation alone selects poorly (arXiv 2510.09738; arXiv 2503.05965). Either add a citation to an example of tuning by correlation, or soften to: "both to evaluate judges and, in some pipelines, to select them."
8. **Appendix A.3, the single-forward-pass logit readout, is not what produced section 4's numbers.** The frozen frontier judges in Experiment 28 returned one forecast per item through chat completions (`experiments/28_public_rating_sets_chaosnli_dices_frontier_judges_ce_vs_pearson_start_23Nov2026/README.md` and `PROTOCOL_11-23-2026.md`), so A.3 describes a method, not a tested result. Say so in one clause, or move it.
9. **Appendix A.5, "the correlation curve flattens."** The flat curves in Experiment 31 are the held-out RPS of Pearson-trained judges. Experiment 31 stores correlations only at the largest budget (`results_11-25-2026.md`, Table 5), where it reports that the cross-entropy fit and the Pearson fit differ by less than 0.05 in every row, which supports the post's point that correlation barely separates them. Proposed text: "the Pearson-trained judge's held-out RPS curve flattens."

## 3. The one caution that changes how strongly a claim can be stated

The post says (section 4) that correlation "has no way to turn [extra ratings] into a better spread or shape" and that every extra rating still has something to teach a proper loss. Both statements are true at the population level. On the real rating sets we tested, though, almost all of the observed advantage of the cross-entropy-trained head is calibration. In Experiment 41 (`experiments/41_output_space_k_sweep_g_d_study_sufficiency_and_label_dollars_start_27Nov2026/README.md`, section 2) the raw advantage reaches 0.128 normalized RPS with the ridge penalty fixed at 0.01 (preregistered; 0.1008 on the views with signal when the penalty is chosen on validation prompts, not preregistered), and once each head's score passes through its own validation-fitted calibration map (one family, fitted by cross-entropy) the remaining difference is at most 0.0024 (0.0022 with validation-chosen penalties) at every K; on every view where the raw gap exceeds 0.01, at most 5.5% of the raw gap remains (at least 94.5% is removed). A small advantage for the cross-entropy-trained head does remain on POPQUORN politeness (about 0.002 at every K), SummEval expert very slightly favors the correlation-trained head under validation-chosen penalties (about 0.001), and a head trained by mean squared error with the same calibration step ties the cross-entropy-trained head within 0.0004. These heads are linear on 24 hashed-text components, so this does not cover judges that learn new features. Experiment 28's DICES result points the same way. The finding supports the post's evaluation claim (correlation cannot see calibration, so a correlation-trained judge scored by a proper rule needs recalibrating) more than a claim that correlation training learns a worse shape.

Proposed sentence for section 4: "One caution from our own controls: on the sets and linear heads we tested, a correlation-trained head followed by a cross-entropy calibration step closes almost all of the gap, so the claim is about what the last optimization step should be, not that ranking is worthless."

## 4. The closing question is now partly answered

The post ends by asking whether to spend a fixed budget on "more items with fewer samples, or fewer items with deeper distributional coverage." For a proper-score target the repository now has a partial answer, with two different parts. (a) Estimating a held-out risk: when a new item costs no more than an extra rating, one rating per item minimizes the variance of the estimate (Lean `free_items_one_rating_optimal` in `experiments/41_output_space_k_sweep_g_d_study_sufficiency_and_label_dollars_start_27Nov2026/lean/AllocationAndClosedForms.lean`, zero `sorry`, only the three standard axioms); with a per-item overhead the optimum is k = max(1, sqrt((item cost / rating cost) × (within-item variance / between-item variance))), a test-side statement, not a training one. (b) Training a cross-entropy head at an equal total number of ratings (Experiment 41, prediction P7, one prompt-level split, linear heads on four to seven public sets): one rating per item was better than or tied with the D-study's k\* in all 20 evaluable cells when the penalty is chosen on validation (16 better, 4 tied) and in 19 of 20 with the penalty fixed at 0.01 (one cell worse, SummEval expert at 512 ratings); P7 as preregistered is therefore not supported in one cell. Cursor's Experiments 39 and 40 point the same direction, but independent reviews found that their headline framings are not supported as written (`qa_of_experiment_39_push_1cd4fc6_11-27-2026/`, `qa_of_experiment_40_push_a4ccb30_11-27-2026/`), so I would not cite them in the post. Proposed sentence, scoped to what was tested: "In our experiments with a proper loss, spending a fixed budget on one rating for each of more prompts did at least as well as spending it on several ratings for fewer prompts; the textbook D-study asks a different question, how reliable each prompt's mean is, and answers it with 2 to 16 ratings per prompt on the public sets we tried."
