**How to read this note.** It reports verification, not a new study. Data: real multi-annotator ratings that Experiment 36 uses (SummEval expert, ChaosNLI SNLI+MNLI, DICES-990, POPQUORN offensiveness and politeness, VeriBench formal specification coverage (FSC) on 0 to 5). Split: Experiment 36's own seed-0 split by prompt group (60/20/20), test items held out. Metric: normalized ranked probability score (RPS), RPS divided by the number of classes minus one, lower is better, and held-out cross-entropy (CE) in nats, lower is better. The reference values are a flat forecast and the training-marginal constant forecast, both in the tables below. A difference always subtracts the same metric on the same test items for two methods, named by how they were trained ("Pearson-trained head", "CE-trained head"); "positive means the CE-trained head is better". Uncertainty: paired, item-clustered percentile bootstrap, 2,000 draws, seed 1, 95% level, from my scripts (Experiment 36's own intervals use 1,000 draws). How the heads were chosen: Experiment 36's linear softmax heads on 24 principal components of a hashed text feature, L2 weight 0.01, L-BFGS-B capped at 35 iterations from zero, seed 0, no validation-based selection; the Pearson-trained head maximizes the correlation of a sampled judge (its variance includes the head's own conditional variance), which is not a convex objective, so its result reflects that optimizer, start and budget. These are not human-judge results for large language models.

## Findings, most important first

1. **The raw proper-score gap is almost all calibration (still present from the earlier audit, now shown on POPQUORN and `native_main` too).** Experiment 36's headline is "the CE-trained head beats the Pearson-trained head on held-out proper scores", for example normalized RPS 0.1595 against 0.2523 on POPQUORN offensiveness. The Pearson objective is blind to the scale and location of the score, so nothing in its training calibrates the output distribution. I passed both heads' scalar mean scores through the same calibration map (a multinomial logit on the standardized mean score and a constant, ridge 1e-4 on the slope, fitted by cross-entropy on the validation ratings that Experiment 36 never reads) and scored the held-out test items. Normalized RPS of the Pearson-trained head minus that of the CE-trained head, positive meaning the CE-trained head is better:
   - SummEval expert (1,280 test items): raw +0.1088 [+0.1023, +0.1155]; matched calibration +0.0016 [+0.0010, +0.0022].
   - ChaosNLI SNLI+MNLI (624): raw +0.0566 [+0.0509, +0.0623]; matched −0.0002 [−0.0006, +0.0001].
   - DICES-990 (198): raw +0.0603 [+0.0467, +0.0743]; matched +0.0008 [+0.0003, +0.0014].
   - POPQUORN offensiveness (300): raw +0.0928 [+0.0814, +0.1039]; matched −0.0000 [−0.0004, +0.0004].
   - POPQUORN politeness (745): raw +0.0703 [+0.0640, +0.0766]; matched +0.0010 [+0.0007, +0.0014].
   - VeriBench FSC `native_main` (20 test items in 5 tasks): raw +0.0397 [+0.0201, +0.0607]; matched −0.0016 [−0.0052, +0.0016].
   The two heads' scalar scores rank the test items almost identically on the five large sets (Spearman 0.926, 0.980, 0.982, 0.961, 0.996) and not on `native_main` (0.573). So on these data the training objective barely changes the ranking; what differs is calibration, which is exactly the thesis (correlation does not identify the distribution), but it is not evidence that proper-loss training finds better rankers. The five VeriBench matrices that need the `R_v1` ratings could not be rebuilt here, so the matched-calibration control covers six of the sixteen matrices. The calibrated heads use extra data (the validation split) that the raw heads do not, so the fair contrast is calibrated against calibrated, the "matched" lines above. The map class is one choice; a richer map could move gaps of this size. The Opus reviewer ran a similar control on the two POPQUORN sets and got matched differences of −0.0000 and +0.0010, which agree with mine.
   - **A correction to my own earlier wording.** I had written that the Pearson-trained head wins its own objective in only 5 of 16 matrices. That is true of the held-out Pearson r of its mean score (the column Experiment 36 reports), but the head is trained on the correlation of a sampled judge, whose variance includes the head's conditional variance. On the six readable sets the Pearson-trained head has the higher sampled-judge correlation on the test items in five: SummEval 0.0216 against 0.0081 for the CE-trained head; ChaosNLI 0.0113 against 0.0035; DICES 0.0153 against 0.0076; offensiveness 0.0099 against 0.0039; politeness 0.0892 against 0.0301; and not on `native_main` (−0.0055 against 0.0512; its optimizer reported `success=False` at the 35-iteration cap). The mean-score correlations, which Experiment 36 reports, are higher or equal for the CE-trained head in 11 of 16 matrices (the Pearson-trained head's is higher in `summeval_expert_binary`, `chaosnli_snli_mnli`, `chaosnli_alpha`, `popquorn_offensiveness` and `veribench_fsc_rv1_expert3`).
