# Ledger: every checkable claim in the "finalized" real-data plan (Gemini, relayed by Brando, 11-26-2026)

**TLDR:** Brando asked me to check the Gemini plan and the Grok/Cursor prompt built from it, and to say what the plan gets wrong. Of 19 claims, 3 are right as written, 10 are right with a note, 2 are imprecise, 2 are wrong, 1 is not supported by any file I could find, and 1 conflicts with this project's primary metric. The mathematics is sound: 21 Lean 4 theorems (zero `sorry`, only `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`) and 51 seeded numeric checks (51 PASS, byte-identical on two runs) back it, and the notes are conditions the plan leaves unstated (adaptive rater counts, the mean versus the sampled readout, greedy decoding is not mean collapse). The data facts are where it is wrong: DICES has 123 (350) and 69 to 76 (990) ratings per item, not 60 to 112; ChaosNLI has 3,113 three-class items, not 4.6 thousand; no file shows an FSC set of 343 items (the anonymized files hold 75 and 100 artifacts with 2 to 4 expert ratings each); HelpSteer2's 2-point rule adds annotators and does not drop items. Cursor and Grok had not pushed anything for this plan when I last fetched (origin/main was still `036cafa`), so Experiment 36 (Cursor's earlier work) was audited instead; see `qa_of_experiment_36_11-26-2026/`.

**Verdict vocabulary** (as in `../claims_audit_11-26-2026/`): CORRECT; CORRECT, WITH A NOTE; IMPRECISE; WRONG; NOT SUPPORTED (no source found in the files searched); CONFLICTS WITH THE PROJECT. **Machine-check label** on every item: *Lean + numerics* (general theorem compiled and the same claim run on many random instances), *numerics only*, *cited* (a published theorem or a statement in a paper, read from the paper), *not checkable*. Nothing is called "proved" or "verified" unless it carries the first label.

**Data behind the numbers.** Part A uses synthetic finite laws only, to check proofs against a known target distribution; it says nothing about human judges. Part B counts the real files (real multi-annotator ratings; no model was trained for it). Reproduce: `check_plan_claims.py` (seed 20261126) and `dataset_facts.py` (see README.md for the command).

## Part A. Mathematics

**M1. Per-sample test risk is unbiased: R̂ = (1/m) Σ_c (1/k_c) Σ_j L(q̂(c), y_cj). CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean + numerics.** Unbiased for any loss, any fixed k_c ≥ 1 (so also one rating per prompt), a different law per prompt, and dependent ratings: it needs only the correct marginal of each rating, and independence changes the variance, not the mean (Lean `plan_estimator_unbiased`, `plan_estimator_unbiased_product`, `instance_unbiased`; N1a to N1f: exact on 240 finite instances, 130 with some k_c = 1, for Brier, RPS, an arbitrary loss table and cross-entropy). **The note:** it is biased when the number of ratings depends on the ratings. With two raters and two more only if they disagree, θ = 0.3 gives E = 129/500 = 0.258, not 0.3 (`adaptive_stopping_biased`, N1g); "a second rater only if the first rated 1" has bias θ(1−θ)(L0 − L1)/2 (N1h). A k_c drawn independently of the ratings is fine (N1i). This bites HelpSteer2, whose annotator count grows when the first annotators disagree.

**M2. Plug-in D_B(p̂‖q) has bias (1 − ‖p‖²)/k. CORRECT. Lean (experiment 36, general) + numerics.** E‖p̂_k − q‖² = ‖p − q‖² + (1 − ‖p‖²)/k exactly (N2a: multinomial enumeration, 70 random (K, k, p, q) with K ≤ 4, k ≤ 6). Zero exactly when p is a point mass (N2c, N2d); with k_c varying across prompts it is the average of (1 − ‖p_c‖²)/k_c (N2e). The Lean theorem is `plugin_DB_bias` in experiment 36 (`SanmiPitch.lean`, line 585). I read its statement: it holds for every K, every n ≥ 1 and every fixed forecast q, over the rationals, with the expectation taken over all n-draw samples from p (the Opus review read it the same way). I did not re-prove it here and I did not recompile that file.

**M3. Exact ERM rate E‖p̂_N − p‖² = (1 − ‖p‖²)/N. CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean (experiment 36) + numerics.** True for the empirical frequency of one context's ratings (N2b exact; N2f in floats for N = 10, 100, 1e3, 1e4, 1e5, worst relative error 1e-10). **The note:** it is not the rate of a trained judge over prompts, which needs a model class. The plan's Python asks for N in {10, 100, 1e3, 1e4, 1e5}; experiment 36's numeric check (`proofs_numeric.py`, `_empirical_rate`) draws 4,000 samples at N = 10, 50 and 200 from one three-class law and accepts a 1% relative plus 0.002 absolute tolerance, and its text (`RESULTS_SUMMARY.md` line 288) gives O(K/N) where the identity gives at most 1/N with no growth in K.

**M4. Anti-properness: for a sampled judge, Corr(X_δ, Y) = √Corr(X_p*, Y) > Corr(X_p*, Y). CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean + numerics.** General Lean theorem on a finite joint law of (c, X, Y): `Model.total_variance`, `Model.corrTrue_eq` (Corr = Var μ / Var Y), `Model.corrCollapsed_eq` (Corr² = Var μ / Var Y for X_δ = a μ + b, a > 0), `Model.anti_proper` (so Corr(X_δ, Y) > Corr(X_p*, Y) when Var μ > 0 and E Var(Y | C) > 0). Both correlations are computed from raw joint moments. N3a to N3e: 150 random models. Every hypothesis is necessary, each shown by a claimed-false case: Y a function of c (no strict gain, both are 1, N3f); a < 0 flips the sign (N3g); a = 0 makes Pearson undefined (N3h); without X ⊥ Y | c the right marginal can have Corr = 1 (X = Y, N3i). **The note:** it concerns a judge whose scored output is a *sample* from its forecast. If the judge is scored on its *expected* score, the maximum correlation (√(Var μ / Var Y)) is attained by the true forecast and by every forecast whose mean is a positive affine function of the true mean (N5a is one: q2 = 0.01 p* + 0.90), and by a point mass at the true mean when the rating grid contains it. Correlation then fails to identify the distribution, and it is indifferent to collapsing rather than rewarding it. "Not identifying" is the accurate phrase for that readout and "anti-proper" is not.

**M5. Greedy decoding is the collapsed judge X_δ (plan, Experiment 1b). IMPRECISE. Lean + numerics for the parts that hold.** The theorem collapses a judge to its conditional *mean*. Collapsing any sampled judge to its own mean multiplies |Pearson| by √(Var X / Var μ_q) ≥ 1 (N3j-a, exact, 120 random pairs, 115 strict). Greedy decoding collapses to the *mode*, and the mode can be anti-ordered to the mean: in the checked example the sampled judge has covariance 9/400 > 0 and the greedy-mode judge has −3/40 < 0 (N3j-b). So "greedy decoding always inflates Pearson" is false, and Experiment 1b must report three readouts of the same model: sampled, expected score, and mode.

**M6. Binary example, p* ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.9} and q2 = 0.01 p* + 0.90: Pearson ties at 0.6532, Brier 0.1433 versus 0.4119. CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean + numerics.** `binary_moments` (Var μ = 8/75, Var Y = 1/4, E Var = 43/300), `binary_pearson_tie` (the tie, √(32/75) between 0.6532 and 0.6533), `binary_brier` (43/300 and 1235707/3000000, ratio between 2.87 and 2.88); N5a to N5e. **Three notes.** (1) The tie is for the probability-as-score readout. For sampled 0/1 draws the squared Pearson is 1024/5625 = 0.1820 for q1 and 1024/19344375 = 0.000053 for q2 (N5b). (2) "Brier" here is (q − Y)². The plan defines ‖q − e_Y‖², which for two classes doubles both numbers (0.2867 and 0.8238) and leaves the ratio at 2.87 (N5d). (3) Scalar L2 on sampled 0/1 draws is improper: the point-mass 0/1 judge scores 7/30 = 0.2333, better than the true forecast at 43/150 = 0.2867 (N5e).

**M7. Ordinal example on {0,…,4}: Pearson 0.5000 versus 0.7071, Brier 4×, RPS 2×, cross-entropy 0.6931 versus +∞. CORRECT. Lean + numerics.** Two equally likely contexts, p* uniform on {0, 2} and on {2, 4}, collapsed forecasts δ₁ and δ₃: Pearson 1/2 and √(1/2); Brier 1/2 and 2; RPS 1/2 and 1 (normalized RPS 1/8 and 1/4); cross-entropy ln 2 and +∞ because the collapsed forecast puts zero mass on every realized rating. `example_moments`, `example_anti_proper`, `example_scores`, `example_ce_support`, `example_ce_true`, `example_ln2`; N4a to N4d.

**M8. Report √RPS and Brier as the proper metrics (plan: "held-out CE, √RPS, Brier, Pearson, Spearman, Kendall"). CONFLICTS WITH THE PROJECT. Lean + numerics for the Jensen step.** `CLAUDE.md` makes normalized individual-rating RPS (RPS divided by K − 1) the primary and common validation-selection metric, with cross-entropy and total variation distance (TVD) as secondary; the plan's panel has neither normalized RPS nor TVD. A square root is a monotone transform, so rankings agree, but differences of √RPS are not risk differences and are not comparable across K. If √ is wanted it must be taken *after* averaging over items: the mean of per-item √RPS is smaller than the √ of the mean RPS whenever the items differ (`sqrt_mean_lt`, `sqrt_mean_example`; N6a on 300 random pairs; N6b: 0.5000 versus 0.7071 for per-item RPS 0 and 1).

**M9. Brier and RPS are strictly proper; CE = entropy + KL. CORRECT. Lean (this repo) + numerics.** Compiled earlier in this repository: `ProperScoring.brier_strictly_proper`, `ProperScoring.thr_excess`, `ProperScoring.rps_strictly_proper` (`extended_checks_11-26-2026/lean/ProperScoring/Basic.lean`; axioms in `BUILD_LOG.txt`). Here N9a (200 random (K, p, q)) and N9b to N9e check E_p[Brier] = ‖p − q‖² + 1 − ‖p‖², E_p[RPS] = Σ_t (F_q(t) − F_p(t))² + Σ_t F_p(t)(1 − F_p(t)), the strictness of both, and cross-entropy = entropy + KL.

**M10. MSE decomposition, standardized L2 = 2(1 − Corr), fair-coin coupling counterexample, independent-judge MSE, and "the optimal independent judge is δ_{μ_Y}". CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean (this repo) + numerics.** `ProperScoring.mse_corr_form`, `ProperScoring.standardized_mse` and, in `../claims_audit_11-26-2026/AuditClaims.lean`, `three_term`, `coins_same_conditional_mean`; here N8a to N8d. **The note:** for an independent judge, E(X − Y)² = (μ_X − μ_Y)² + σ_X² + σ_Y² (N8d), and on the integer grid {0,…,4} the minimizer is a point mass at the grid point nearest μ_Y, not δ_{μ_Y}; the exact-mean mixture is no better, and δ_{μ_Y} needs μ_Y to be a grid point (N8e: 80 random laws, 74 with μ_Y off the grid).

**M11. "Pearson is invariant to affine maps." CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Lean + numerics.** Only for a positive slope: `ProperScoring.corr_affine_invariant` has the hypothesis 0 < a; a < 0 flips the sign (N7b) and a = 0 is undefined.

**M12. An M-sample rollout pmf has Brier excess (1 − ‖q‖²)/M over its own forecast; the single-pass logit readout has none (plan, Experiment 4). CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Numerics only.** N10a: exact for (K, M) in {(6,1), (6,5), (3,20), (4,12)}; N10b: the excess falls like 1/M and never reaches 0 (0.5400, 0.1080, 0.0270 for M = 1, 5, 20). **The note:** the plan does not say whether a rollout writes a rationale before the score. If it does, the rollout samples y from Σ_r q(r | c) q(y | c, r), which is a different conditional from the direct pass q(y | c); the two agree only under an assumption nobody states and that fails in general for language models. Experiment 36 does state it (`pitch_addenda.py`, copied into `SANMI_PITCH.md` line 218: the M chain-of-thought samples "are a Monte Carlo estimate of the same marginal" that the direct pass reads). So treat "direct logits" and "rationale rollouts" as two judges and compare them as such. A linear head sampled M times only demonstrates the identity above. (Derivation; not machine-checked; the Opus review reached the same conclusion.)

## Part B. Data and citations (counts printed by `dataset_facts.py`, saved in `results/dataset_facts.out`)

**D1. FSC "R_v1 (343 items, 0–5 ordinal), ~5 domain experts/item". NOT SUPPORTED. Counts from anonymized files + cited.** The sister repo's anonymized files give the old set 75 artifacts over 25 tasks with 195 expert ratings (30 artifacts with 2 ratings, 45 with 3) and the new set 100 artifacts over 25 tasks with 247 ratings (55 with 2, 43 with 3, 2 with 4; 180 of the 247 are 5s). Pooled: 175 artifacts, 50 tasks, 442 ratings. A five-rater panel gives 345 ratings on the old artifacts (45 artifacts with 5 ratings, 30 with 4), cited from the sister repo's reconciliation, which says it found no 343 in the files it searched. Consequences: FSC supports k ≤ 3 on the old set (at most 5 on the five-rater panel), so a sweep to k = 8, 16, 32 is impossible there; use the sister repo's frozen task splits (10 train, 5 validation, 10 test tasks per set) and keep rater identity out of every public file.

**D2. SummEval: 1,600 summaries (100 articles × 16 systems), 3 experts and 5 crowd workers, 4 aspects, 1–5. CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Counts + cited.** Confirmed from the file (sha256 matches the pin in experiment 36). **The note:** the SummEval paper says experts did a second round on scores that differed by more than 2 points, and were allowed to see the other experts' first-round scores; Krippendorff α rose from 0.4132 to 0.7127. Expert ratings are therefore not independent draws. The unbiasedness in M1 survives (it needs marginals only) but the target is the post-discussion distribution, and the variance of R̂ is understated by an iid model.

**D3. POPQUORN: politeness 6.7 and offensiveness 8.7 raters per item; $12 per hour, 13 minutes per 50 ratings, about $0.05 per rating. CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Counts + cited.** The files match Table 1 of the paper: offensiveness 1,500 items, 13,036 ratings, 262 annotators, mean 8.69 (5 to 10 per item); politeness 3,718 items, 25,042 ratings, 506 annotators, mean 6.74 (3 to 8). The paper states $12 per hour and, in the offensiveness section only, that the "medium time" (presumably the median) of finishing 50 annotations is 13 minutes, so $0.052 per rating is arithmetic for offensiveness (12 × 13/60 / 50), not a published price, and the paper gives no timing for politeness. The k-sweep ceiling is 8 to 10. The public repo is `Jiaxin-Pei/Potato-Prolific-Dataset` (experiment 36 looked for it under another name and wrote that it could not be found).

**D4. DICES "60–112 raters/item". WRONG. Counts.** DICES-350: 350 items, 123 raters, every item has 123 ratings (104 after the README's 19-rater quality filter). DICES-990: 990 items, 172 raters, 69 to 76 ratings per item (60 to 69 after the 12-rater filter); 72,103 rows as released, 64,886 after the filter. No file shows a count of 112.

**D5. ChaosNLI "3-class (4.6k items) & 2-class". WRONG. Counts.** The three-class set is SNLI 1,514 plus MNLI-matched 1,599 = 3,113 items. The 4,645 total includes 1,532 two-class abductive-NLI items. Every item has 100 votes (the "100 votes per item" in the plan is right).

**D6. HelpSteer2 "filtered out high-disagreement items >2 points". IMPRECISE. Cited (dataset card read line by line; checks in `dataset_facts.py`).** The 2-point figure is real, but it decides something else. The card says every sample got at least three annotators and up to five "if the initial annotators do not agree with each other sufficiently (2 points or less on helpfulness)" (the wording is the card's, and garbled), and that the released label is the rounded mean of the three annotators who agree most (the average number of annotators is 3.41). That drops outlier *annotations*, not items. The only item-level filtering the card states is a quality-assurance step that keeps 20,324 samples, and it names no disagreement criterion. The per-annotator lists are released in a separate `disagreements` split, so the variance the plan wants to footnote is recoverable. The plan's 0 to 4 scale, five attributes and "3+ annotators" are right (a small share of samples keep only two annotations because some were not ratable). The Opus review labelled this claim WRONG; the card has the number, so I call it imprecise, and the correction is the same.

**D7. Kohli (arXiv:2605.29797) "already runs subsampling saturation curves on ChaosNLI and DICES-990". CORRECT, WITH A NOTE. Cited (full text of v2 read).** One author (Guneet Kohli, Apple), v1 28 May and v2 29 Oct 2026. The paper's tables show KL capturing 91±3% of the improvement at N = 10 annotators on ChaosNLI against 74±6% for entropy correlation, and on DICES-990 at N = 3, 90% against 62%. **The note:** its "correlation" is *entropy correlation* (whether the model ranks items by how much humans disagree); the trained models are DeBERTa-v3-base (with a RoBERTa cross-check) on hard, label-smoothed or soft labels; there is no Pearson-trained judge in it. So it motivates collecting more raters per item, and does not test correlation-trained against loss-trained judges. Experiment 36 reports the 91±3% and the 90%-versus-62% figures correctly and says it does not repeat the fine-tune; its section title writes "Kohli et al." for a single-author paper (a nit). The Opus review read only the abstract; I read the full text of v2, so these table numbers are now checked.

## Part C. What the plan leaves out or under-specifies (standing requirements in `CLAUDE.md`)

**G1. The primary and secondary metrics.** Add normalized RPS (primary, selection metric) and TVD (secondary) to the panel; keep CE; write "ranked probability score (RPS)" in full; report √ only after averaging, if at all (M8).

**G2. A fair correlation-trained competitor.** In experiment 36 the committed Pearson head is trained with the same L2 penalty of 0.01 as the cross-entropy head and reaches a training correlation of 0.032, 0.014 and 0.026 on SummEval experts, ChaosNLI and DICES-990, against 0.250, 0.151 and 0.192 with no penalty (reproduced by me). A plan that trains "5 objectives" must select each objective's hyperparameters on the validation split with the same budget, or the result measures the tuning and not the objective.

**G3. Matched calibration.** Pearson does not identify scale or offset, so a correlation-trained judge's raw proper scores are arbitrary. Give both heads the same validation-fitted temperature and class offsets before comparing proper scores. In experiment 36 this removes almost all of the gap: the held-out normalized RPS of the Pearson-trained head minus that of the CE-trained head falls from +0.109, +0.057, +0.060 to +0.0025, −0.0005, +0.0009 on SummEval experts, ChaosNLI and DICES-990 (reproduced by me with my own script and with the agent's; paired item-clustered bootstrap, B = 1000, positive means the CE-trained head is better). The honest reading is that the correlation objective leaves calibration unidentified, which supports the thesis, and that at this model size a calibrated Pearson-trained head and a CE-trained head are close.

**G4. Informative features.** Experiment 36's heads use 24 hashed-text SVD components; the CE head beats the constant train-marginal forecast by only 0.0113, 0.0040 and 0.0059 nats of held-out cross-entropy (0.0019, 0.0009, 0.0022 normalized RPS). Until the features carry signal (frozen LLM embeddings or rating-token logits), the comparison is close to "calibrated constant against miscalibrated constant".

**G5. Paired intervals and the validation split.** Every claimed difference needs a paired item-clustered interval on the same metric and items; experiment 36 builds `x_val` and never reads it, and its protocol note says it was written before the first test metric although the protocol and the results arrived in the same commit (`7b3c7d1`).

**G6. Split hygiene.** ChaosNLI is split by uid, but 123 of 624 test items share a premise with a train item (reproduced twice); group by premise. For FSC use the sister repo's frozen task splits and record prior exposure.

**G7. Real language-model judges.** The plan asks for open-weight 7B to 32B judges, rank reversals between real judges, greedy versus distributional readouts on one model, and a researcher-and-GPU dollar axis. None of that is in the repository yet; the linear-head matrix is a toy.
