# Derivations for experiment 35 (extended checks): why training on correlation is not training on the Brier score

Notation follows Appendix B of the 11-25-2026 memo: p is the true rating distribution of an item, q is the judge's forecast, ratings are 0 to 5 (K = 6), mu(x) is the true conditional mean rating and m(x) the forecast's mean rating. Every claim lists the check in `check_math.py` (ids N1 to N25, printed in `results/check_math_output.txt`) and the Lean theorem in `lean/ProperScoring/` that proves it, when there is one. Experiments 33 and 34 already check the standardized-score identity, the MSE decomposition across items, the Pearson factorization through the conditional mean and the logit gradients; this note cites them and does not repeat their numerics.

The motivating question is whether correlation is a proxy for squared error, and so close to the Brier score, which is proper. The short answer is that correlation is squared error after a free, oracle choice of level and scale, and that free choice is exactly what makes it improper. The headline claims are C6 to C12 (correlation) and C13 (training). C1 to C5 are compact background.

## Part A. Correlation is not the Brier score (the headline)

**C6. Squared error splits into location, scale and correlation.** For any two variables with finite second moments, the mean squared difference equals the squared difference of their means, plus the squared difference of their standard deviations, plus two times the product of the standard deviations times one minus the correlation. For variables standardized by their own mean and standard deviation, the first two terms vanish and what is left is two times one minus the correlation. Proof: expand, writing the covariance as the correlation times the two standard deviations, and complete the square in the standard deviations. Intuition: squared error charges a judge for three separate faults, being shifted, being stretched or squashed, and pointing in the wrong direction. Correlation only looks at the third fault. So the guess in the brief is right in form, with the one correction that the identity holds for any pair of variables, and the clean "two times one minus rho" form needs each variable standardized by its own statistics. Checks: N14 (and experiment 33, B.6). Lean: `mse_cov_form` (covariance form, no division), `mse_corr_form`, `standardized_mse`.

**C7. Correlation is blind to level and scale.** For any positive a and any b, the correlation of a times X plus b with Y equals the correlation of X with Y. Proof: centering removes b, and a cancels between the covariance and the standard deviation. Intuition: a judge that says 4.6 whenever the truth is 2.3, or one that crams everything between 2.4 and 2.6, can still have perfect correlation. Check: N15. Lean: `corr_affine_invariant`, `corr_self`.

**C8. The best correlation is the correlation ratio, attained exactly by positive affine images of the conditional mean.** Over all functions f of the item, the largest correlation of f(X) with the rating Y is the square root of the variance of the conditional mean over the variance of Y. Proof: by the tower rule the covariance of f(X) with Y equals the covariance of f(X) with mu(X); Cauchy-Schwarz bounds it, with equality exactly when f(X) is a positive multiple of mu(X) plus a constant, almost surely. This needs the conditional mean to vary; if it is constant every f has correlation zero. Intuition: correlation identifies the conditional mean only up to an unknown shift and stretch, and for a numeric 0-to-5 rating it cannot see the shape of the rating distribution at all. Checks: N16, and experiment 33 (B.2). Lean: not formalized.

**C9. Correlation is mean squared error after an oracle affine recalibration fitted on the evaluation data.** The minimum over a and b of the mean squared error of Y against a times X plus b equals the variance of Y times one minus the squared correlation. Proof: least squares, with slope equal to the covariance over the variance of X. Intuition: reporting correlation is like letting the judge see the test ratings, refit its own shift and stretch on them, and then scoring the squared error. That forgives miscalibration entirely, and on a small test set it is optimistic because two parameters are fitted on the same data they are scored on. One precision the brief missed: this identity involves the squared correlation, so with a free slope sign it also forgives a judge that is anti-correlated. Correlation itself keeps the sign, which corresponds to the recalibration with a nonnegative slope. Check: N17. Lean: `oracle_affine_mse` (lower bound for every a, b, and attainment).

**C10. Correlation is not a scoring rule.** A scoring rule in the Gneiting and Raftery sense averages a per-item score of the forecast and the observed rating. Under any such average, changing one item's forecast changes the total by an amount that depends only on that item. Correlation fails this: the same change to item 1 (forecast 0.2 to 0.9 with rating 0) moves correlation by minus 0.6513 in one context and minus 0.6600 in another. It is also undefined for one item and for constant forecasts. Intuition: correlation depends on sample means and spreads across the whole set, so "proper" in the scoring-rule sense does not even apply to it. Its improperness is better stated as: the expected-score maximizers include wrong forecasts (C8). Check: N18. Lean: not formalized.

**C11. Equal correlation, different Brier score (exact).** Five binary items with true probabilities 1/10, 3/10, 1/2, 7/10 and 9/10. Judge A reports the truth. Judge B reports one half plus one tenth of the truth minus one half, a squashed version. Both have correlation exactly one. The mean expected two-category Brier score is 17/50, which is 0.34, for A and 587/1250, which is 0.4696, for B; cross-entropy is 0.5130 against 0.6627 nats. Intuition: correlation cannot tell a calibrated judge from a timid one. Check: N19. Lean: `N19_equal_corr`, `N19_different_brier`.

**C12. Ordering reversal (exact).** Four binary items with truth 1/5, 2/5, 3/5 and 4/5. Judge C is the squashed forecast, from 0.47 to 0.53, with correlation exactly one. Judge D is calibrated but noisy, 1/4, 7/20, 13/20 and 3/4, with squared correlation 81/85, a correlation of about 0.976. Yet the expected Brier score is 481/1000 for C and 81/200 for D, so the judge with the higher correlation is strictly worse on the proper score. The ordinal version uses three items on ratings 0 to 5: judge C mixes each true distribution one to nine with the uniform distribution, so its mean ratings are an exact affine image of the true means and its correlation is one; judge D keeps two items exact and moves one-tenth of item 3's mass from rating 5 to rating 4, giving correlation 0.99926. Mean expected RPS is 6823/37500, about 0.18195, for C and 97/750, about 0.12933, for D, against 193/1500, about 0.12867, for the truth. Intuition: a ranking by correlation and a ranking by a proper score can disagree in both directions, so correlation cannot stand in for the proper score when choosing a judge. Checks: N20, N21, figure 3. Lean: `N20_corr_C`, `N20_corr_D_lt_one`, `N20_brier_reversal`; the six-category RPS example is checked in exact rationals in Python only.

**When treating correlation as a squared-error proxy is right.** By C6, rankings by correlation and by mean squared error agree among judges whose mean and spread already match the human ratings, for example judges that are calibrated in level and scale, or judges that are recalibrated with an affine map fitted on held-out ratings before scoring. In the binary case the mean is the whole distribution, so after a good recalibration correlation-trained and squared-error-trained judges should nearly coincide. Correlation remains a sound diagnostic of linear association and of ranking. The honest claim is therefore not "correlation is wrong", but: correlation is not a proper score; it is squared error after an oracle affine recalibration, so it is blind to calibration and to everything beyond the conditional mean.

## Part B. Training with correlation (the second headline)

**C13. The correlation objective has no gradient along level or scale, so data do not set them.** Write the Pearson gradient with respect to the vector of forecast means. Its inner product with the all-ones vector is zero, and its inner product with the centered forecast is also zero, because the gradient is proportional to the standardized truth minus rho times the standardized forecast. Proof: both inner products reduce to n rho minus rho n. Intuition: moving every forecast up, or stretching them all, does not change correlation, so a gradient method never receives a signal about where the level and scale should be; they stay wherever initialization, the optimizer and regularization leave them. Check: N25 (and experiment 33 for the gradient itself). Lean: not formalized.

**Demonstration (synthetic data, no human ratings).** A linear-logit softmax judge over ratings 0 to 5 is trained by L-BFGS-B with cross-entropy, Brier, squared error on the numeric rating (MSE) and Pearson correlation of the forecast mean with the rating (the same expected-rating correlation used for the Pearson-trained judge in experiment 02), from three random starts and two data seeds, at N from 10 to 100,000 ratings. A fifth arm takes the Pearson fit on 70 percent of the data, fits an affine map of its mean on the other 30 percent, and tilts each forecast exponentially to the new mean. The metric is the mean total variation distance (TVD) to the known true distribution on 4,000 fresh items, lower is better, range 0 to 1. With a well-specified class, the median TVD at N = 100,000 is 0.0037 for the CE-trained judge and 0.0044 for the Brier-trained judge, while the Pearson-trained judge stays between 0.420 and 0.524 across runs, and its mean rating is off by a root mean squared error of 1.03 rating points. Held-out recalibration fixes the mean (root mean squared error 0.008) but not the distribution (TVD 0.342). The MSE-trained judge also recovers the mean (0.006) but not the shape (TVD 0.396). Intuition: correlation training gets the ordering right and nothing else; squared error gets the mean; only a proper score on the whole distribution gets the distribution.

**How this squares with the memo's binary control (experiment 29).** In the K = 2 control of experiment 29 (`RESULTS_11-23-2026.md`, line 16), the squared-error-trained judge is within plus 0.0003 ranked probability score (RPS) of the CE-trained judge, with an interval that includes zero; the Pearson-trained judge is worse in all seven setups at the largest budget; and after one shared one-dimensional calibration the gap shrinks to between 0.0002 and 0.0043 RPS. The derivations predict exactly this pattern. With two categories, squared error on the 0-or-1 rating is half the Brier score (C3), which is proper, so squared error and CE should tie. Pearson leaves level and scale unset (C7, C13), so the raw Pearson-trained judge is miscalibrated and loses. A recalibration repairs the level and scale, and with two categories the mean is the whole distribution, so most of the gap closes; what remains reflects whatever non-affine distortion the trained judge has and the finite calibration data. With six categories, as in the synthetic demonstration, recalibration cannot repair the shape, so the gap stays large.

## Part C. Background, kept compact

**C1. Brier is strictly proper.** For a categorical rating with true law p, the expected Brier score of forecast q equals the squared Euclidean distance between q and p plus one minus the sum of squared p. The second term does not depend on q, so p is the unique minimizer, for each item separately. The identity uses only that p sums to one. Intuition: the Brier score is the squared distance to the truth plus a fixed noise floor. Checks: N1, N4, N8, figure 1. Lean: `brier_excess`, `brier_strictly_proper`.

**C2. Consistency in N, separate from propriety.** Propriety is a population statement; consistency is about samples. With finitely many item types and no model restriction, the empirical Brier risk of a forecast equals its squared distance to the empirical frequencies plus a constant, so the empirical frequencies are the unique empirical minimizer, and by the strong law of large numbers they converge almost surely to p. Cross-entropy behaves the same way, because expected CE is the entropy of p plus the Kullback-Leibler divergence from p to q, and its empirical minimizer is again the empirical frequency. For a parametric class, consistency of the empirical-risk minimizer needs the usual conditions (a compact parameter set or convexity, a uniform law of large numbers, and a unique population minimizer); this is a sketch, and no source was opened to verify a specific theorem statement. Intuition: proper means the target is right; consistency means enough data reaches it. Checks: N2, N5, N6, and the CE and Brier arms of figure 2. Lean: the empirical-minimizer step is `brier_strictly_proper` applied to the empirical frequencies; the almost-sure convergence step through Mathlib's strong law was not formalized.

**C3. RPS is strictly proper.** RPS is the Brier score of the cumulative distribution, so its excess is the sum over thresholds of the squared gaps between the two cumulative distributions, which vanishes only when every cumulative value agrees, and that fixes every probability by successive differences. Checks: N3, N4. Lean: `thr_excess` (one lemma for every threshold-Brier score, which covers both Brier and RPS), `rps_strictly_proper`.

**C4. Propriety is an infinite-capacity statement.** When the forecast class cannot contain the truth, different proper scores choose different best forecasts. In the binomial family on three ratings with truth 0.5, 0.1, 0.4, CE picks theta 0.45 exactly, Brier picks about 0.2435 and RPS picks about 0.4185. In the misspecified training demonstration, CE and Brier plateau at different TVDs, 0.0792 and 0.0699. Intuition: a proper score is a promise about what happens when the truth is reachable; with a restricted judge, the choice of score matters. Checks: N7, N23.

**C5. Squared error, Gaussian noise and the apparent contradiction.** For a numeric outcome with finite variance, expected squared error equals the squared distance to the mean plus the variance, for any noise distribution, so squared error is strictly consistent for the mean, with no Gaussian assumption. It is not a proper score for the whole distribution: two forecasts with the same mean get the same expected squared error. Squared error is also the Gaussian negative log-likelihood with known variance, and that is where the Gaussian reading belongs: Gaussianity matters for the likelihood interpretation, for efficiency and for using the fit as a predictive distribution, not for recovering the mean. So there is no contradiction: propriety is always relative to a target, the mean or the full distribution, and to a forecast family. For a categorical rating, squared error on the one-hot vector is exactly the Brier score, which is proper for the full distribution; squared error on the numeric 0-to-5 rating sees only the mean. In the exact six-rating example, the truth (1, 2, 7, 7, 2, 1)/20 and the forecast (7, 2, 1, 1, 2, 7)/20 both have mean 5/2 and both have squared-error risk 5/4, but their Brier scores are 73/100 and 109/100, their RPS 3/25 and 24/125, and their CE 1.4950 and 2.6625 nats. Checks: N9 to N13. Lean: `l2_decomposition`, `l2_unique_minimizer`, `l2_onehot_eq_brier`.
