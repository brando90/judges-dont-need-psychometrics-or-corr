# Does matching expectations recover a discrete distribution? Indicator means, the mean rating, Brier and correlation

**Doc link:** <https://github.com/brando90/judges-dont-need-psychometrics-or-corr/blob/main/experiments/35_brier_vs_corr_math_l2_pearson_lean_start_26Nov2026/extended_checks_11-26-2026/discrete_means_EXPLAINER.md>

**TLDR:** For a discrete rating the K indicator means are the probabilities, so matching all K of them recovers the distribution (that part of the statement is right). The single mean rating is one number: it fixes the distribution at K = 2 and leaves K - 2 shape directions open at K >= 3. Next-token cross-entropy is the vector version, not correlation. Brier is a different object from correlation even at K = 2, because correlation ignores level and scale.

Date: 11-26-2026. Data: synthetic only, no human ratings. Every number below is printed by `discrete_means_check.py` into `results/discrete_means_output.txt`; its last line is `SUMMARY 22 PASS, 2 FAIL out of 24 checks`, and the two FAIL lines are explained in "What did not pass". Check ids (DM1, DM6d and so on) point to lines of that file.

## The intuition, in plain words

Start with the part that is true. When a rating can take only a few values, the average of "was the rating a three" is exactly the chance of a three. So a model that gets all of those averages right, one for each possible rating, has the whole distribution. That is a definition, and it holds without any mixing of continuous and discrete settings.

The care is needed about which average is meant. The mean rating is a different object: one number, the ratings weighted by their probabilities. With two possible ratings one number is enough, because the mean rating is the chance of the high rating. With six possible ratings, zero to five, there are five free probabilities and the mean rating pins down one combination of them, so four directions stay open. In the check, two laws had the same mean, two and a half, and the same variance, one and a quarter. One was the familiar count of heads in five fair coin flips, the other put all its weight on the ratings zero, two, three and five. Forecasting the first when the second is true makes no error in the mean at all, yet every proper score notices: the excess cross-entropy is about four tenths of a nat, and almost a third of the probability is in the wrong place.

A separate problem sits on top of that, and it appears even with two ratings. Correlation cannot see level or scale. Picture a forecaster who ranks the days perfectly, every rainy day gets a bigger number than every dry day, but who says ten percent when the truth is seventy, and thirty when the truth is ninety. Their correlation with what happened is perfect. Brier is not: it charges for the gap between the number and the outcome. Or think of a thermometer that reads twenty degrees low. Its readings rise and fall in step with the real temperature, so the correlation is one, and you would still not trust it to choose your coat. In the two-rating check, a model trained on correlation did reach the best correlation available, but its probabilities were the true ones stretched and shifted by an amount that depended on where training started, a scale anywhere from about four tenths to about one and three tenths. Its distance from the truth stayed just under one tenth even at a million ratings, while the Brier-trained and cross-entropy-trained models were off by about one thousandth. A held-out sample used only to fit one straight-line correction brought the correlation-trained model back to the same level. So that gap is level and scale, and it is repairable.

Now the language model. Its next-token loss is cross-entropy over a finite vocabulary, which is exactly the vector version: one indicator average per token, matched with a proper loss. It is not correlation. Try the alternative, correlating the expected token number with the observed token number. Token numbers are arbitrary labels, so the model learns only the average label and nothing about which token is likely. In the fifty-token check its distance sat near seven tenths whether it saw a hundred thousand tokens or ten million, while cross-entropy's fell from about seven hundredths at ten thousand tokens to about two thousandths at ten million. Shuffle the labels and cross-entropy does not change, but the best achievable correlation does.

Ratings tempt us because they are ordered, so a correlation looks natural. It still compares one number per item. A correlation for each rating value does better, because every rating keeps its ranking across items, but each probability is then fixed only up to its own stretch and shift. You get rankings, not probabilities. Fit one straight line per rating value on held-out ratings, renormalize, and you land on cross-entropy's level. That is why correlation plus a calibration step comes out close to cross-entropy in practice, and why the calibration step is doing the work.

## The thirty-second version

If a rating takes only a few values, the average of each value's indicator is the probability of that value, so matching all of those averages gives the whole distribution. That is what next-token cross-entropy does for a language model. The mean rating is different: it is one number. With two ratings it fixes the distribution; with six it leaves four directions open. Correlation has a second, separate blind spot. It ignores level and scale, so a forecaster who ranks every day correctly but says ten percent for seventy still scores perfectly. Brier charges for that gap, so it is not correlation, even with two ratings. Training on correlation gives you rankings. Add a calibration step fitted on held-out ratings, one straight line per rating value, and you get most of the way to cross-entropy. That is why the two look close in practice.

## The three verdicts, precisely

**A. Is "matching expectations recovers the distribution" correct?** Correct for the vector of K indicator means, incorrect for the single mean rating once K >= 3. Each indicator mean equals P(Y=k | x) exactly (DM1: maximum absolute error 0 in exact arithmetic over 1000 random K = 6 laws; 8.4e-04 over 4e5 Monte Carlo draws), the K indicator means sum to one so there are K - 1 free numbers, and matching all of them leaves 0 free directions at K = 2 and at K = 6 (DM2). The mean rating leaves 0 free directions at K = 2, 1 at K = 3 and 4 (= K - 2) at K = 6 (DM2). Two laws with the same mean 5/2 and the same variance 5/4 differ by excess cross-entropy (CE, equal to the Kullback-Leibler divergence) 0.40321 nats and total variation distance (TVD) 0.31250 while the mean error is 0 (DM3); their indicator means differ, P_A = [1/32, 5/32, 5/16, 5/16, 5/32, 1/32] against P_D = [1/12, 0, 5/12, 5/12, 0, 1/12].

**B. Is a language model "just doing correlation"?** No: next-token CE over a finite vocabulary of V tokens is the vector version, matching all V indicator means with a proper loss, and correlation of a scalar is a different objective that learns only the mean. In the V = 50 toy, CE-trained TVD to the true next-token distribution fell from 0.0677 at N = 1e4 to 0.0022 at N = 1e7 (log-log slope -0.500, DM9a), while training on the correlation between the expected token ID and the observed ID learned the mean ID up to an affine map (R^2 >= 0.99997) and left TVD at 0.698 at N = 1e5 and 0.695 at N = 1e7 (ratio 1.00, DM9b). Token IDs are arbitrary labels: over 8 relabelings CE TVD varied by 2.1e-08 while the best achievable correlation ranged over 0.140 to 0.191 (DM9c). For ordered ratings correlation looks plausible but is still one scalar per item. Per-class correlation (one correlation per rating value) keeps every ranking (smallest within-class Spearman correlation 0.9930, DM8a) but identifies each probability only up to an increasing affine map, so at K = 6 and N = 600,000 its TVD is 0.0593 against CE's 0.0034 (DM8b); K affine maps fitted on held-out ratings plus renormalization bring it to 0.0033 (DM8d).

**C. Is Brier the same as training with correlation?** No, even at K = 2. Pearson correlation is unchanged by a + b f with b > 0 and Brier is not: every forecast q = a + b p has correlation 1.000000 with p, yet its expected Brier excess is mean((a + (b - 1) p)^2), zero only at (a, b) = (0, 1) (DM4: 0.0096 to 0.0775 for the four non-truth examples printed, against 0 for the truth). The general identity is E[(X - Y)^2] = (mu_X - mu_Y)^2 + (s_X - s_Y)^2 + 2 s_X s_Y (1 - rho), and the squared difference of standardized scores is 2(1 - rho) (DM5, errors about 1e-14). At K = 2, Brier is exactly twice the squared error on the mean rating and twice the ranked probability score (RPS) (DM5b, error 0). Trained from random initializations at K = 2 and N = 1,000,000, the Pearson-trained model's learned probability is an exact affine image a + b p of the truth with b in [0.394, 1.285] and a in [-0.203, 0.245] depending on the initialization (R^2 >= 0.99997, DM6b); the Brier-trained and CE-trained models land on the truth (b = 1.0023, a = -0.0016, DM6c). Median TVD is 0.0988 for the Pearson-trained model against 0.0013 for both Brier and CE (ratio 78, DM6d), and 0.0012 for the Pearson-trained model after a held-out affine calibration (DM6e).

### What is imprecise in the statement as worded

- "Matching expectations" has to say which expectation. A vector of K - 1 free expectations (the indicator means) recovers the distribution; a single scalar expectation (the mean rating) does so only at K = 2. The dividing line is vector versus scalar, not continuous versus discrete. The moment statement counts the same way: K - 1 moments, the same count as K - 1 indicator means, determine a K-point law, and it is the first moment alone that falls short at K >= 3.
- "Matching" has to say with which objective. Squared error against the indicators is Brier and is proper. Correlation matches only up to a + b f, so even the right indicator averages are not enforced in level and scale.
- Inside a one-parameter family (for example the binomial family in experiment 34) the mean pins the distribution by assumption. That is a restriction on the model class, not a property of discrete ratings.

### What each training objective pins down

- Squared error on the mean rating: fixes the level and scale of the mean (K = 6: root mean squared error of the learned mean at most 0.0070), not the shape (TVD 0.3302 raw, 0.2318 after calibration, DM8e).
- Pearson correlation of the mean rating: fixes neither (TVD 0.2653 raw, 0.2296 after calibration, against CE's 0.0034, DM8e).
- Per-class Pearson correlation: fixes every class ranking, not level and scale (TVD 0.0593 raw, 0.0033 after calibration, DM8b and DM8d). With a softmax output its K slopes coincide within a run (largest max/min ratio 1.018, DM8c); with independent sigmoid outputs the raw outputs sum to between 2.369 and 3.107 per context instead of 1.
- Brier and CE: fix both; trained numerically here (DM6c, DM8b). The ranked probability score is also strictly proper by theory but was not trained numerically here.

There are two separate gaps. The level-and-scale gap exists even at K = 2 and is repaired by calibration on held-out ratings. The shape gap (K - 2 directions) exists only at K >= 3 and calibration of the mean cannot repair it.

## How to read the numbers

- **Data:** every number comes from synthetic ratings drawn from known true distributions, with a fixed design (the same number of ratings per context). N is the number of synthetic training ratings. TVD is measured on the training contexts against the known truth, because a table model has one free logit vector per context and has no held-out contexts. "Held-out" in "held-out affine calibration" means a second synthetic sample of the same size used only to fit the calibration (class probabilities mapped by a least-squares affine map, clipped at 1e-6 and renormalized). No human ratings and no prior-exposure question arise.
- **Metric:** TVD = 0.5 * sum over ratings of |learned probability - true probability|, averaged equally over contexts; range 0 to 1, lower is better; 0.05 means 5 percent of the probability mass is misplaced; the sampling-noise reference is the CE-trained value at the same N (0.0013 at N = 1,000,000 for K = 2). CE is the average of -log of the probability given to the observed rating (lower is better); Brier is the sum over ratings of the squared difference between the forecast probability and the 0/1 indicator (lower is better); RPS averages the squared difference between forecast and observed cumulative probabilities over the K - 1 cuts (lower is better); Pearson is the correlation between forecast and outcome (higher is better, unchanged by a + b f with b > 0).
- **What is compared:** all comparisons are the same metric (TVD to the truth) on the same synthetic data for models named by how they were trained (the Pearson-trained model, the CE-trained model, and so on). "Excess" scores subtract the score of the true distribution from that of the forecast on the same metric, so they are at least 0, with 0 meaning the forecast is right. The ratio 78 is Pearson-trained TVD divided by CE-trained TVD at N = 1,000,000, K = 2.
- **Uncertainty:** no confidence intervals, because the truth is known and the quantity is an exact distance. Medians are over random initializations and data draws, with min to max ranges printed (for example the K = 2, N = 1,000,000 Pearson-trained TVD is 0.0988 with range 0.04969 to 0.1804 over 20 converged runs of 24).
- **How the models were chosen:** a table model whose class contains the truth, so only the objective, the initialization and the optimizer path decide the answer; L-BFGS from random initial logits with standard deviation 0.1, 0.3, 1.0 or 3.0 (K = 2 and K = 6) or 1.0 or 2.0 (V = 50), up to 60,000 iterations; analytic gradients checked against finite differences (maximum relative error at most 3.4e-09). The correlation objectives are not convex and their optimum set is a family of affine images of the truth, so the reported correlation-trained results reflect this optimizer, these starts and this budget, not a guaranteed best. CE and Brier reach the truth from every start. A correlation run counts as converged when its in-sample correlation is within 1e-4 of the best achievable; stalled runs are counted and excluded from that objective's medians, which favors correlation.

## What did not pass, and what it means

Two checks print FAIL. Both are optimizer-convergence diagnostics for a prediction (P3) that I revised after the first run, not claims about the statement under test, and no tolerance was loosened to change them.

- **DM6a** (K = 2): every Pearson run with initialization standard deviation at most 1.0 should reach the best in-sample correlation. Result: largest gap 5.86e-03 over 108 such runs (tolerance 1e-4); one of the 108 stalled, and 27 of 36 runs at standard deviation 3.0 stalled (28 of 144 overall, largest gap 0.089).
- **DM8f** (K = 6): every correlation-type run with standard deviation at most 1.0 should reach the best in-sample correlation. Result: largest gap 3.53e-04 over 216 such runs; one stalled (Pearson of the mean rating). Overall stalls: per-class Pearson (softmax) 22 of 96, per-class Pearson (sigmoid) 6 of 96, Pearson of the mean rating 9 of 96, almost all at standard deviation 3.0.

Meaning: the correlation objective sometimes stops at saturated outputs where the gradient vanishes. This does not touch A, B or C: the identities (DM1 to DM5b) are exact and all pass, and the converged runs show the affine-image structure (DM6b, DM8a). Excluding stalled runs is conservative for the claim that correlation-trained probabilities are mis-scaled; the K = 2 median at N = 1,000,000 is 0.0988 with or without them.

## Limits and surprises

- Synthetic data, table models, one noise structure. Nothing here is a statement about human ratings or any particular neural judge.
- A surprise: in the well-specified two-parameter logistic control (truth inside the model class) the converged Pearson fit sits near the truth (logit (-0.523, 1.489) against (-0.5, 1.5) at N = 100,000, DM7 observation) and training is fragile (4 of 12 runs reach the best correlation at N = 100,000). The scale-and-shift freedom therefore needs a model class or judge read-out flexible enough to realize affine images of the truth, as the table model does; it is not a property of every model class.
- Consistency with the memo (neural judges on real ratings, different data, not replicated here): the K = 2 squared-error judge is within +0.0003 test RPS of the CE judge (interval -0.0021 to +0.0028), the test RPS of the Pearson-trained judge minus that of the CE-trained judge is +0.0132 to +0.0761 in all seven setups (positive means the CE-trained judge is better), and after one shared one-dimensional calibration the gap shrinks to 0.0002 to 0.0043 (`paper_latex_and_notes/ICML_format_tech_memo_11-25-2026/sections/06_lora.tex`). This study offers a mechanism for that pattern, not a test of it.

## TL;DR

Matching all K indicator means with a proper loss (Brier, CE, RPS) recovers a discrete distribution; matching the one mean rating does so only at K = 2, and correlation, which ignores level and scale, does not even at K = 2. A language model's next-token CE is the vector version, and correlation plus a calibration step looks close to it only because the calibration step restores what correlation discards.
