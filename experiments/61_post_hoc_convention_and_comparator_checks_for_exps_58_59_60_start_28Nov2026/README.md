# Experiment 61: post-hoc convention and comparator checks for Experiments 58, 59 and 60

**Doc link:** <https://github.com/brando90/judges-dont-need-psychometrics-or-corr/blob/main/experiments/61_post_hoc_convention_and_comparator_checks_for_exps_58_59_60_start_28Nov2026/README.md>

**TLDR:** Three independent reviews of Experiments 58 to 60 raised three questions about a convention or a comparator that the pre-registered analyses did not fix. Each now has a number, none reverses a pre-registered result, and all three narrow what the paper may say. (1) Experiment 60's "collapsed forecast" skill on DICES-350 is $-0.48$ only for a point mass at the label nearest the mean; it is $-0.25$ at the modal label, $-0.24$ at the median label and $-0.15$ for a forecast with the same mean and the least spread, always below the base rate, against $+0.17$ for the truthful forecast. (2) A judge whose forecast equals each item's human rating distribution, read as a hard label, is already $9.8$ points below the human rater-share rate of unsafe on DICES-350 ($9.5$ on DICES-990), which is $39$ to $48\%$ of the five judges' hard-label errors on DICES-350 ($58$ to $84\%$ on DICES-990). (3) Experiment 59's selector that uses three-fold cross-validated recalibrated proper risk had higher regret than a uniformly random pick of one of the five judges in $11$ of $20$ cells ($9$ with the whole interval above $0$); correlation selection did in $1$ of $20$ ($0$ significant) and the raw proper score in $0$ of $20$.

## What was found (answer first)
1. **Experiment 60's collapsed-forecast skill depends on how a mean is turned into a forecast, its sign on the DICES sets does not.** Skill is one minus the risk of a forecast over the risk of the half-A base rate on the held-out half B of each item's ratings (higher is better, $0$ is the base rate); intervals are item-clustered 95% intervals. Normalized ranked probability score, DICES-350: truthful forecast $+0.167$ $[+0.142, +0.192]$; point mass at the label nearest the mean $-0.475$ $[-0.535, -0.416]$ (Experiment 60's frozen convention, reproduced); point mass at the modal label $-0.245$ $[-0.298, -0.190]$; at the median label $-0.237$ $[-0.290, -0.185]$; two adjacent labels with the same mean as the half-A law $-0.152$ $[-0.197, -0.107]$. DICES-990: truthful $+0.148$ $[+0.128, +0.168]$; nearest-label $-0.492$; modal $-0.275$; median $-0.273$; mean-preserving $-0.190$ $[-0.225, -0.153]$. The nearest-label forecast sits on the middle label "Unsure" for $56\%$ of DICES-350 items and $53\%$ of DICES-990 items; the modal label is never "Unsure". On alphaNLI (two labels, Brier) the truthful forecast has skill $+0.644$, every point mass $+0.528$ to $+0.529$, and the mean-preserving forecast equals the truthful one, because with two labels the mean is the whole law. Truthful minus collapsed skill, same score, same half B, is positive with the interval above $0$ for every convention on every set, except the mean-preserving forecast on alphaNLI, where the difference is exactly $0$ because the two forecasts coincide.
2. **Part of the hard-label error in Experiment 58 is a difference between two human quantities.** The human rater-share rate is the mean over items of the share of ratings in the event; the human majority rate is the share of items whose most likely human label is in the event (ties split). A judge that knows each item's human distribution exactly and is read as a hard label is off by their difference. Primary events, majority rate minus rater-share rate in percentage points with item-clustered 95% intervals: ChaosNLI SNLI $-1.6$ $[-3.2, +0.0]$, MNLI-m $+5.2$ $[+3.0, +7.5]$, alphaNLI $-0.4$ $[-2.1, +1.3]$, DICES-350 $-9.8$ $[-12.7, -7.1]$ ($32.7\%$ rater-share against $22.9\%$ majority for the unsafe event "Yes"), DICES-990 $-9.5$ $[-12.1, -6.9]$ ($28.2\%$ against $18.8\%$). For the secondary event "Unsure or Yes" the gap is $-16.1$ and $-14.5$. The five judges' hard-label errors against the rater-share rate on DICES-350 are $-25.2$ to $-20.4$ points, so the perfect-judge gap is $39$ to $48\%$ of them; against the human majority rate they are $-15.4$ to $-10.6$ (every interval below $0$). On DICES-990 the errors are $-16.2$ to $-11.2$ against the rater-share rate and $-6.8$ to $-1.8$ against the majority rate (Claude Opus 5.5's interval includes $0$). Over the $25$ primary (set, judge) cells, the error against the majority rate is smaller in absolute value than against the rater-share rate in $12$.
3. **Experiment 59's pre-registered proper-score selector was often worse than choosing a judge at random.** Regret is the skill lost by not picking the judge with the best calibrated test risk, in percentage points of skill (lower is better). The random pick has the mean regret of the five judges. Path B (lowest three-fold cross-validated recalibrated proper risk on the labeled items) had a mean regret above the random pick's in $11$ of $20$ (set, budget) cells, with the paired $95\%$ interval above $0$ in $9$: ChaosNLI SNLI at $25$ ($+2.08$ points), MNLI-m at $25$ and $50$ ($+2.54$, $+1.31$), DICES-350 at $25$, $50$, $100$ ($+4.71$, $+2.25$, $+0.98$) and DICES-990 at $25$, $50$, $100$ ($+3.79$, $+1.44$, $+0.35$). It was better than random with the whole interval below $0$ in $8$ cells (alphaNLI at $50$, $100$ and $200$; SNLI and MNLI-m at $100$ and $200$; DICES-990 at $200$). Path A (Pearson correlation) had a mean regret above the random pick's in $1$ of $20$ cells ($0$ significant) and was significantly better than random in $17$. Path C (raw proper risk, no calibration inside the selection; a sensitivity run in Experiment 59) was never above the random pick and significantly better in $19$ of $20$. So Experiment 59's finding that correlation selection had lower regret than path B in all $20$ cells is partly a statement about path B, a selector that fits a calibrator on two thirds of $25$ to $200$ single ratings per candidate; it is not evidence that no strictly proper score can select a judge well, because the raw proper score did better than correlation on all eight DICES cells (Experiment 59).

## Limits
- C1 is an oracle check on empirical rating laws: no language model is involved, and the "forecasts" are built from the ratings themselves. It shows how large the difference between conventions is on three real rating sets; it does not say which convention a deployed judge uses. The mean-preserving forecast is one choice among many that have the mean $\mu$.
- C2 is descriptive: it uses the human ratings and the frozen forecasts, fits nothing, and the "perfect judge" is hypothetical. The gap depends on the event and the rating scale; all ratings of each item are used (exactly $100$ per ChaosNLI item, $123$ per DICES-350 item, $69$ to $76$ per DICES-990 item, counted from the frozen bundle). The tie rule (a tie in the most likely label is split equally) affects $0.2$ to $1.2\%$ of items.
- C3 inherits Experiment 59's limits: five judges at temperature $0$, in-domain labels, at most $200$ single ratings (label budgets capped at the pool size by its amendment A1, so the largest DICES-350 budget uses $175$ items), and intervals over the $200$ pool/test splits (they cover the choice of the labeled pool, not the sampling of items). A uniformly random pick among five judges is one comparator; it is not a claim that practitioners choose at random.
- All ratings of these sets were seen in earlier experiments. The analyst had also seen the reviewers' scratch numbers for C1 (modal label) and C3 (11 of 20) before this experiment; the script recomputes both from scratch and agrees with them.
