## Label: this is an ORACLE CHECK, not evidence about language-model judges
No language model is involved. The "judges" are built from half of the human ratings of each item and scored against the other half. The experiment applies a theorem to the empirical rating laws of three real rating sets and shows how large its two sides are.
The theorem is Lean-checked for finite laws (squared form `rho^2 < rho` in Experiment 36; square-root form `PlanAudit.Model.anti_proper` in `experiments/35_brier_vs_corr_math_l2_pearson_lean_start_26Nov2026/extended_checks_11-26-2026/gemini_plan_claims_audit_11-26-2026/PlanClaims.lean`, port `Sampled.Model.anti_proper` in `docs/notes/point2_support_27Nov2026/lean/Point2/SampledReadout.lean`; Lean 4.36.0, Mathlib `fc4f59e5`, zero `sorry`, standard axioms only).
Statement: if a judge's score S is drawn from the human rating law p*(. | c) independently of the human rating Y given the item c, then Pearson r(S, Y) = rho, where rho = Var(mu(C)) / Var(Y) is the between-item share of the variance of a single rating and mu(c) is the conditional mean rating; a judge that outputs mu(C) has r = sqrt(rho), which is larger whenever 0 < rho < 1.
Hypotheses: independence of S and Y given the item, positive between-item variance of mu, positive within-item variance.

## What was found (answer first)
1. **The two readouts of the same rating law differ by 0.15 to 0.23 in Pearson correlation, in the direction the theorem says.** Correlation is measured against held-out single human ratings (half B). The truthful judge that samples its score from the half-A rating law has r = 0.651 on alphaNLI, 0.192 on DICES-350 and 0.181 on DICES-990;
   the judge that outputs the half-A mean has r = 0.802, 0.424 and 0.401. The difference "r of the mean judge minus r of the sampled judge" is +0.151 [0.140, 0.163], +0.232 [0.226, 0.236] and +0.220 [0.214, 0.225] (item-clustered 95% intervals, 4,000 bootstrap draws). H1 is supported on all three sets.
2. **The measured correlations equal the theory to better than 0.001, which is expected.** The between-item share of rating variance, estimated from all ratings with the finite-rater noise removed, is rho_hat = 0.651 [0.618, 0.682] (alphaNLI), 0.192 [0.166, 0.217] (DICES-350) and 0.181 [0.160, 0.201] (DICES-990). The sampled judge's correlation differs from rho_hat by at most 0.0002 on every set;
   the mean judge's correlation differs from its finite-half prediction by at most 0.0003 (the large-sample value sqrt(rho_hat) is 0.807, 0.438 and 0.425, slightly above the measured 0.802, 0.424 and 0.401 because each half has finitely many raters). H2 is supported on 3 of 3 sets.
   **Say plainly in the paper that H1 and H2 are checks of the algebra and the code on real rating laws, not independent empirical tests:** the split-half cross-product is an unbiased estimator of the between-item variance, which is exactly the correction inside rho_hat, so agreement to this precision is a consequence of the construction (the protocol states this before the run).
   The one independent check is the Monte Carlo draw of sampled scores on 20 of the 200 splits per set: its correlation minus the closed form has mean -0.0018, -0.0009 and -0.0008 and a largest absolute value of 0.0076, 0.0107 and 0.0138, which is what one draw per observation gives.
3. **What the experiment adds is the size of the effect on real rating laws.** About 35 percent of the variance of a single alphaNLI rating is within-item disagreement, and about 81 percent on DICES-350 and 82 percent on DICES-990. On DICES a judge that reproduces the human rating law exactly has Pearson 0.19 against a single human rating when its score is read as a sample,
   while the judge that collapses to the mean reaches 0.42, and the collapsed judge is the worse forecast: its matched-rule skill (1 minus its risk over the human base-rate risk; 0 is the human base rate) is -0.475 [-0.535, -0.416] on DICES-350 and -0.492 [-0.539, -0.444] on DICES-990, against +0.167 [0.142, 0.192] and +0.148 [0.128, 0.168] for the truthful forecast.
   On alphaNLI both are above the base rate: +0.644 [0.611, 0.676] truthful, +0.529 [0.476, 0.582] collapsed. The risk of the collapsed forecast minus the risk of the truthful forecast (positive means the truthful forecast is better) is +0.0571 [0.0463, 0.0683] in Brier units on alphaNLI and +0.1470 [0.1380, 0.1567] and +0.1359 [0.1259, 0.1456] in normalized ranked probability score (RPS) units on the DICES sets. H3 is supported on all three sets.

## Method in one paragraph
Sets and coding (read-only, from `experiments/39_*/data/processed/study_a_bundle.npz`): alphaNLI (400 items, 100 ratings each, binary), DICES-350 (350 items, 123 ratings each, ordered No < Unsure < Yes coded 0, 1, 2) and DICES-990 (400 items, 69 to 76 ratings each, same coding). ChaosNLI SNLI and MNLI-m are excluded because their labels are unordered, so a Pearson correlation with an arbitrary numeric coding means nothing.
For each set and each of 200 random splits (seed `SeedSequence([20261128, 60, set_index, split])`) the ratings of every item are divided at random into half A (floor(n/2) ratings, which build the judges) and half B (the rest, the held-out human ratings); no rating is in both halves. The truthful judge draws, for each half-B observation, a score from the item's half-A rating distribution; its expected correlation is computed in closed form from moments
(and checked by Monte Carlo on 20 splits, seeds `SeedSequence([20261128, 60, 77, set_index, split])`). The mean judge outputs the item's half-A mean. Pearson r is taken over all (item, rating) observations of half B, which is the quantity in the theorem, and is NOT the item-level correlation between a judge's expected label and the human mean label used in Experiments 46 and 57 (for a judge that outputs the exact mean that item-level correlation would be 1).
Proper-score side: the matched rule is Brier on alphaNLI and normalized RPS on DICES (the same primary rules as Experiments 57 to 59); the truthful forecast is the half-A distribution floored at 1e-6, the collapsed forecast is the point mass at the grid label nearest the half-A mean (halves round up; this is the theorem's point mass when the mean is on the grid and a hard-label approximation otherwise), and the base rate is the pooled half-A histogram;
all are scored against half B by the unbiased per-sample risk (mean over items). Uncertainty: item-clustered bootstrap, 4,000 draws, percentile 95% intervals, seed `SeedSequence([20261128, 60, 99, set_index])`; the per-item sufficient statistics are computed for each split and the statistic is averaged over the 200 splits inside every draw, so the intervals cover item sampling given the rater-split average and not the finite number of raters of the human population.

## Scope and limits
This is a statement about Pearson correlation of sampled scores under the stated conditions. It is not a claim that correlation is wrong in general: a judge that outputs the conditional mean is exactly what correlation rewards, and the real-judge experiments point the same way: in Experiment 46 none of 50 Pearson-versus-proper-score rank reversals of the preregistered test is significant, and in Experiment 59 choosing among five frozen judges by correlation had lower regret than choosing by a cross-validated calibrated proper score in all 20 cells (the raw proper score did better than correlation on DICES).
The point-mass forecast is a hard-label forecast (the majority label on alphaNLI), so panel (b) of Figure 1 also compares a soft forecast with a hard one, as Experiment 58 does for pass rates. The sampled judge is an idealization (real judges are not samplers of the human law); the theorem gives its correlation as the benchmark for what a perfectly truthful forecaster looks like to Pearson.
The ratings of these sets were seen in earlier work (Experiments 28, 46, 48, 55 to 59). Lean covers the finite-law theorem; the empirical agreement here is numerical.
