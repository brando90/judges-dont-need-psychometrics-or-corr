**Correction, 11-27-2026. Read this before the verdict below.**

An independent review of the first version of this experiment found four problems in Study A. I fixed them, wrote the corrected protocol down and committed it before re-running, and re-ran Study A. The old headline does not survive. Here is what changed.

1. **The old headline is withdrawn.** It said the proper-score selector significantly lowers held-out RPS against the per-item D-study rule in 10 of 20 dataset-budget cells. After the fixes it beats the most-raters rule in 2 of 20 cells (6 of 20 before the multiplicity correction) and it beats the most-items rule in none.
2. **What still holds.** For held-out RPS, spending a fixed rating budget on more items and fewer raters per item is better in 18 of 20 cells by the mean. It is statistically solid in only 5 of 20 cells after the correction, and the largest gain is 3.0% of total RPS (13% of the part of RPS that a forecast can still reduce). So: a small, real-looking tilt toward more items, not a rule.
3. **Why the old number was too good.** First, the selector was allowed to read the full rating pool of its pilot items, which is more data than the 80 items by 16 ratings it was described as using. Second, the pilot itself holds 1,280 ratings, which is 2.5 to 20 times the budget being allocated, and that cost was never charged. Third, the confidence intervals ignored the sampling of test items and there was no correction for 20 comparisons. Fourth, ties in the oracle were broken in favor of more items, which flattered every selector that also prefers more items.
4. **What the corrected run tests.** A fixed most-items rule, a fixed most-raters rule, a real D-study that picks the smallest number of raters per item whose estimated reliability reaches 0.80 or 0.90, and the proper-score selector, all seeing only the same 80 by 16 pilot table. The pilot cost is reported next to each budget and is not subtracted, so any win by a pilot-using selector is an upper bound on its real value.
5. **The two old "D-study selectors" were not tests.** At a fixed budget the old per-item rule always picked the most raters and the old grand-mean rule always picked the most items, whatever the data said. They are formulas, and I now label them as such. Their ordering on item-mean error (20 of 20 cells) and on grand-mean error (18 of 20) is a property of those formulas, not a data-driven result.
6. **The decision rules I set in advance.** A pilot-based proper-score selector would have added value only if it beat the most-items rule in at least one cell. It beat it in none. A pilot D-study would have added value only if it beat both fixed rules in at least one cell. It did not (the most-items rule beat it in 3 cells). All six pre-declared predictions held. I wrote them after seeing the old results and the review, so they test that the corrected protocol behaves as the review implied; they are not blind forecasts.
7. **Smaller fixes to the text.** The RPS formula in the first protocol left out a forecast-independent term, so the absolute levels contain a floor of 0.09 to 0.19, and paired differences were never affected. alphaNLI has two categories, not three, and the NLI labels are nominal, so RPS depends on the order imposed; cross-entropy and total variation distance, which do not, show the same pattern. The sentence "selection accuracy falls to near chance" for Study B was wrong: near the crossovers the REML pick matches the oracle in 50% to 64% of replications, a coin flip between two nearly tied designs, and a wrong pick there costs about 3% of the error variance. The claim that a small pilot can be significantly worse than equal allocation is weakened: six of 55 cells look worse, none survives a correction for 55 comparisons, and four survive a softer one.
8. **What I would still say in the paper.** Proper scores make the target of an allocation explicit; they do not choose an allocation by themselves. For the held-out-forecast target on this grid, more items mildly beats more raters, and a 1,280-rating pilot did not pay for itself against that fixed rule. If the target is a per-item universe score, the formulas still favor more raters per item.
9. **What this correction does not cover.** One forecast head (27 recalibrations of five frozen judges), one pilot size, a grid with at least 2 raters per item and at most 64 items, ChaosNLI without rater identities, and a bootstrap that does not resample the development pool. Study B (the synthetic REML check) was not re-run; only its wording changed. A core-Lean check of its closed forms (the 4 by 4 design is uniquely best for t strictly between 4/15 and 11/15) compiles with no `sorry` and only the three standard axioms.
