# What does each rating contribute to cross-entropy and Pearson training? | 11-21-2026

Date: 11-21-2026

**TLDR:** Cross-entropy rewards the probability assigned to each observed rating. Pearson correlation of expected ratings uses one number per artifact and measures linear association across artifacts. A nonlinear judge can achieve perfect Pearson correlation while getting the rating probabilities wrong. More ratings help cross-entropy distinguish alternative distribution shapes when those ratings cover artifacts where the shapes differ.

## One artifact and one observed rating

**Write down what information the judge receives and whose rating it forecasts.** Let $x_i$ contain the inputs available at deployment, such as a problem, a candidate solution and a reference solution. Write $p_i(r)=\Pr(Y=r\mid X=x_i)$ for the target distribution over ratings $r\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ and $q_i(r)$ for the judge's distribution. A target for an individual rater conditions on rater identity when it is available. A target for a population averages over the specified rater population.

Suppose artifact $i$ has $k_i>0$ observed ratings $y_{ij}$. Each artifact receives the same weight among $n$ artifacts. Its empirical rating frequencies are $\hat p_i(r)$, its observed mean is $h_i=\sum_r r\hat p_i(r)$ and its predicted mean is $m_i=\sum_r r q_i(r)$.

## Cross-entropy gives a separate loss for each observed rating

**If the human chose rating $y$, the loss is $-\log q_i(y)$.** Predicting probability $1/2$ incurs $\log 2$ natural-log units (nats). Adding a second artifact leaves this first loss unchanged. Lower cross-entropy (CE) is better, with range $[0,\infty]$.

$$
L_{\rm CE}
= \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac1{k_i}\sum_{j=1}^{k_i}-\log q_i(y_{ij})
= -\frac1n\sum_i\sum_r\hat p_i(r)\log q_i(r).
$$

The minus sign makes this the loss to minimize. A term with zero target probability contributes zero. Forecasting zero for an event of positive target probability gives infinite expected CE. Averaging the negative log probability over a fresh rating gives

$$
\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_i}[-\log q_i(Y)]
= \underbrace{H(p_i)}_{\text{loss of the true forecast}}
+ \underbrace{D_{\rm KL}(p_i\Vert q_i)}_{\text{excess loss}}.
$$

The Kullback–Leibler divergence $D_{\rm KL}$ is nonnegative, with equality only at $q_i=p_i$. This equality identifies the true distribution when it is an available forecast. In a restricted family, expected CE picks a minimizer of average divergence within the family. If $l_{ir}$ is a softmax logit, the derivative of $L_{\rm CE}$ with respect to $l_{ir}$ is $(q_i(r)-\hat p_i(r))/n$. Each coordinate compares its forecast and observed frequencies directly. [Gneiting and Raftery, *Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation*](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)

## Pearson gives an exact term per artifact after standardization

**The squared difference between the two standardized means is the requested “loss term.”** Define $\bar m=n^{-1}\sum_i m_i$ and $s_m^2=n^{-1}\sum_i(m_i-\bar m)^2$, and likewise $\bar h,s_h$. With $s_m,s_h>0$, write $z_i=(m_i-\bar m)/s_m$ and $t_i=(h_i-\bar h)/s_h$. Expanding the squares and using $\sum_i z_i^2=\sum_i t_i^2=n$ gives

$$
\rho=\frac1n\sum_i z_i t_i,\qquad
\boxed{\,L_{\rm Pearson}=1-\rho=\frac1n\sum_i\frac{(z_i-t_i)^2}{2}\,}.
$$

Pearson correlation ranges from $-1$ to $1$, higher is better. Its loss ranges from $0$ to $2$, lower is better. A constant forecast or constant target makes Pearson undefined. A stabilizer added to its denominator defines a different objective.

Every summand uses means and standard deviations computed across the whole dataset. Changing a single prediction can change every summand. Training with the average of minibatch correlations can therefore optimize a different objective. Two expansions expose the category-level terms on the notebook page. Since $\sum_i t_i=\sum_i z_i=0$,

$$
\rho
=\frac{1}{n s_m}\sum_{i,r} t_i\,r\,q_i(r)
=\frac{1}{n s_h}\sum_i\frac{1}{k_i}\sum_j z_i(y_{ij}-\bar h).
$$

In the first expression, category $r$ has a coefficient proportional to its numeric value, and the denominator $s_m$ changes with the forecasts. In the second expression each observed rating appears, but the ratings for an artifact enter only through their average. Correlating the repeated forecasts with individual observed ratings would change the denominator to include within-artifact variation and could also change the artifact weights.

**The gradient shows exactly which changes Pearson can detect.** Differentiating the centered dot product and its normalizer gives

$$
\frac{\partial L_{\rm Pearson}}{\partial m_i}
=\frac{\rho z_i-t_i}{n s_m},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\partial L_{\rm Pearson}}{\partial l_{ir}}
=\frac{\rho z_i-t_i}{n s_m}\,q_i(r)\,(r-m_i)}.
$$

The second equality uses $\partial m_i/\partial l_{ir}=q_i(r)(r-m_i)$. The scalar discrepancy multiplying every category for an artifact is shared. In probability coordinates, any feasible perturbation $d_i$ with $\sum_r d_i(r)=0$ and $\sum_r r d_i(r)=0$ leaves $m_i$ unchanged. Pearson remains *exactly* unchanged for every feasible step in this direction. With six categories and an interior distribution, these two independent constraints leave four shape directions per artifact. A shared parameterization can restrict which directions the judge can realize.

The linear operation is $q_i\mapsto \sum_r r q_i(r)$. Pearson then takes a *normalized* dot product of centered means. This normalization is nonlinear in the forecasts. The functions $x\mapsto q(r\mid x)$ and $x\mapsto m(x)$ can themselves be arbitrarily nonlinear within the judge's model family. For example, the nonlinear mean $2+x^2$ below achieves perfect Pearson correlation with the true means.

## What infinite data identify

**In the population, maximizing Pearson against individual ratings identifies their conditional mean up to a positive affine transformation.** Let $Y$ be an individual rating, $\mu(X)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X]$ and $m(X)$ a scalar judge. Assume the variances of $m(X),\mu(X),Y$ are finite and positive. The conditional residual $Y-\mu(X)$ has zero covariance with every square-integrable function of $X$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Corr}(m(X),Y)
= \operatorname{Corr}(m(X),\mu(X))
  \sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mu(X))}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}.
$$

The square-root factor is the attainable upper bound over all scalar judges. Equality requires $m(X)=a+b\mu(X)$ almost surely for some $b>0$, by equality in Cauchy–Schwarz. A restricted family must contain such a function to attain the bound. If evaluating against the exact conditional mean, the upper bound is $1$. Against individual ratings it falls below $1$ whenever raters can disagree at a fixed $X$ with positive probability. If $\mu(X)$ is constant and $Y$ has positive variance, every nonconstant judge has zero population correlation with $Y$.

If the reference is the mean of $k$ conditionally independent ratings from the same population, replace $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ by $\operatorname{Var}(\mu(X))+\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)]/k$. More repetitions reduce noise in the reference mean. They leave the positive affine ambiguity and the shape directions intact. A non-independent rater panel requires its covariance structure.

## A six-category example with a nonlinear mean

**Two judges can disagree about a particular rating by a factor of three while their predicted means match on every artifact.** Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$, $U=(1/6,\ldots,1/6)$, $e_r$ put probability one on $r$, and $d=(0,1,-2,1,0,0)$. Define two fully specified forecasts

$$
b_x=U+\frac{2x^2-1}{10}(e_5-e_0),\quad
p_x=b_x+\frac{\mathbf 1\{x=1\}}{24}d,\quad
q_x=b_x-\frac{\mathbf 1\{x=1\}}{24}d.
$$

Both forecasts have positive entries, sum to one, and have mean $2+x^2$. With $p$ as truth, both earn Pearson and Spearman correlation $1$ against the true means across the three artifacts (average ranks for ties). The two forecasts also receive identical correlations against any observed means for which the coefficient exists. At $x=1$, the true probability of rating $2$ is $2/24$, while $q$ assigns $6/24$. The artifacts $x=-1$ and $x=1$ have the same true mean but different true distributions. Even unlimited training data cannot recover those two distributions from the true mean alone without access to additional artifact information.

For this *specified* example, the excess expected CE of $q$ over $p$ at $x=1$ is $(10\log(5/3)-2\log 3)/24\approx0.121293$ nats. The normalized **ranked probability score (RPS)** is the average squared error of five cumulative probabilities against the indicators $\mathbf 1\{Y\le j\}$ for $j=0,\ldots,4$. Its range is $[0,1]$, lower is better. If $P_x,Q_x$ are the two cumulative distributions, subtracting the true forecast's expected RPS cancels the rating noise and leaves $\frac15\sum_{j=0}^4(Q_x(j)-P_x(j))^2=1/360$ at $x=1$. The **total variation distance (TVD)**, half the sum of the absolute probability differences, equals $1/6$ there, versus zero for the true forecast. TVD ranges from $0$ to $1$, lower is better. The other two artifacts contribute zero to all three differences. These are exact population comparisons of fixed forecasts. They involve no training, random split or sampling interval. The finite-budget simulation has its own generator and estimates.

On human data, individual-rating CE and RPS estimate expected losses from single observed ratings. Empirical TVD compares to a finite histogram. With just one observed rating, its expectation is $1-\sum_r p(r)q(r)$, minimized by putting all mass on a mode. With sparse ratings, distinguish empirical TVD from TVD to the population. If the intended use is ranking, Spearman correlation of the means measures rank agreement. A strictly increasing transformation of the predicted means preserves their ranks. If the intended use is a numeric expected rating, squared error targets that mean and signed bias reports systematic over- or underprediction. Accuracy of a single predicted category targets a modal category. [Project evaluation definitions](../../02_exp06_ce_vs_pearson_matched_budget_start_09Nov2026/EVALUATION_CRITERION_11-09-2026.md)

## How many artifacts versus how many ratings per artifact?

**CE can distinguish two positive, fully specified competing distributions once the data include enough observations where those distributions differ.** Suppose the alternatives are $p_x,q_x$ with identical means, and $p_x$ generates the data. Draw $n$ independent artifacts from the same feature distribution under both alternatives. Conditional on their features, draw $k$ independent ratings per artifact. Pearson of predicted means ties for these alternatives on every nondegenerate sample. CE compares log likelihoods. Conditional on the features, the *expected* total log-likelihood advantage for $p$ is $k\sum_{i=1}^n D_{\rm KL}(p_{x_i}\Vert q_{x_i})$.

Define the affinity $B(x)=\sum_r\sqrt{p_x(r)q_x(r)}\in(0,1]$. Let $\Lambda$ be the likelihood of all ratings under $q$ divided by their likelihood under $p$. For a wrong choice, $\Lambda\ge1$. Markov's inequality and independence give

$$
\Pr_p(\text{CE selects }q)
\le \mathbb E_p[\sqrt{\Lambda}]
= \boxed{\bigl(\mathbb E_X[B(X)^k]\bigr)^n}.
$$

This upper bound shrinks as the candidates become easier to tell apart. When $B(X)$ varies, Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb E[B(X)^k]\ge(\mathbb E B(X))^k$. For a fixed count $nk$, the bound for one rating on each of $nk$ independent artifacts is at least as tight as the bound for repeated ratings on $n$ independent artifacts. This compares bounds for two simple alternatives. Other learning problems can require repeated ratings to distinguish item variation from rater variation.

**Missing an informative artifact can place a hard limit on what any learner can do.** Suppose $p_x$ and $q_x$ differ only on an artifact subtype of probability $\pi$. With equal prior probabilities for the alternatives, even the minimum possible average wrong-choice probability is at least $(1-\pi)^n/2$. With probability $(1-\pi)^n$ all training features miss this subtype, and then the data have exactly the same likelihood under both alternatives. The equal-prior optimal error is also at most $\frac12(\mathbb E B(X)^k)^n$, since $\min(P,Q)\le\sqrt{PQ}$ for the two complete data likelihoods.

In the six-category example, $\pi=1/3$. For two training artifacts with two ratings apiece, enumerating all 11,664 feature-and-rating sequences gives optimal error $0.416152263$ between the lower bound $0.222222222$ and upper bound $0.478651439$. These figures describe an exact finite decision problem with equiprobable alternatives. They supply no universal CE crossover or estimate for the human-rating study. If both candidates imply the same distribution or a Pearson tie rule supplies the true shape, the identification argument predicts no CE advantage over that choice.

## What if “correlation of the distributions” means correlating their probabilities?

**The indexing decision changes which information the objective uses.** Specify which numbers form the paired observations before interpreting $\operatorname{Corr}(q,p)$.

| Paired observations | Information retained | An exact ambiguity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One pair $(m_i,h_i)$ per artifact | Predicted and observed mean ratings | Every feasible mean-preserving change to the forecast ties, as does a common positive affine change to predicted means. |
| All $6n$ component pairs $(q_i(r),p_i(r))$ flattened | Category shape and variation across artifacts | Every forecast and target average $1/6$ over the stacked components. For $0<a<1$, $q_i=U+a(p_i-U)$ has flattened Pearson $1$ if the stacked target has positive variance. |
| Six component pairs within each artifact, then average the artifact correlations | Within-artifact category shape relative to uniform | $q_i=U+a_i(p_i-U)$ with any $0<a_i<1$ has profile Pearson $1$ on each nonuniform target profile. Each artifact can use its own $a_i$. A uniform forecast or target makes its profile Pearson undefined. |

The latter two objectives can detect discrepancies that preserve the expected rating. Each can still reward an incorrect forecast with maximal correlation, so neither gives a strictly proper distribution score. Substituting sparse empirical frequencies for $p_i$ introduces another source of variation. The simulation uses only the first definition for Pearson training.
