## Answer first
1. **The two results are both right: they used different judge pools.** The local Codex run selected among 15 recent frontier-model judges with native probabilities (pool N15). The real_n_sweep run selected among 44 (or 5) older judges that output 3-vote histograms (pools H44, H5). On the same 75 artifacts, same ratings:
   - N15: CE selection beats Pearson selection under **both** protocols (point estimate favors CE in 9/9 Codex-split cells and 12/12 real_n_sweep-split cells; interval excludes 0 in 6/9 and 5/12).
   - H44: Pearson selection beats CE selection under **both** protocols (Pearson better in 9/9 and 12/12 cells; interval excludes 0 in 7/9 and 7/12). H5: Pearson better or tied (6/9 and 12/12 points, no interval excludes 0).
2. **The split protocol and the ratings budget are not the cause.** Moving a pool from the Codex splits (250 splits, whole families held out, 3/7/15 training families) to the real_n_sweep splits (400 task splits, 5 test tasks, 4/8/12/16 training tasks) never flips a sign. Using 1, 2 or all training ratings per artifact moves the Pearson-minus-CE gap by at most 0.003 RPS for N15 (Codex splits) and 0.012 for H44 (real_n_sweep splits).
3. **Why the pool decides it (measured on all 75 artifacts, in-sample):**
   - N15: 8 of 15 judges beat the constant forecast on RPS (best 0.1217 vs constant 0.1737); judge ranking by correlation agrees with ranking by RPS (Spearman 0.93 across judges). The pool also holds judges whose correlation is mediocre but whose forecasts are badly calibrated (three codestral judges: RPS 0.41 to 0.44, 88% to 93% of items put floor probability 1e-6 on an observed rating). With 3 training families, Pearson picks 15 different judges over the 250 splits and its pick lands 0.077 RPS above the best judge on the same test families; CE's pick lands 0.044 above (11 different judges).
   - H44 and H5: **no judge beats the constant** (best RPS 0.1793 vs 0.1737). 85% to 87% of judge-item pairs put floor probability on an observed rating, median CE 10.3 nats (H44). CE selection is then mostly counting zeros from 3-vote noise; Pearson at least picks the best-correlated judge, H2_opus (r = 0.777), which is also the best on RPS and CE.
   - Union of both pools (59 judges): Pearson picks H2_opus (highest r of all, 0.777, vs 0.760 for the best native judge) but its RPS is 0.1793, worse than constant; CE picks a native judge (RPS 0.1244). CE selection wins by +0.022 to +0.074 RPS in all 21 cells (interval excludes 0 in 12/21). That is the clean case of the page's point: correlation cannot see calibration, CE can.
4. **Tested and rejected: "it is the 3-vote format."** I re-expressed the 15 native judges as simulated 3-vote histograms (3 votes drawn from each native distribution, 20 independent draws, same floor 1e-6; label: simulated). CE keeps the advantage in all 21 cells (+0.027 to +0.001, smaller than with native probabilities; interval excludes 0 in 6/21 cells, the small training sizes). So the sign comes from which judges are in the pool, not from the output format; the format only shrinks CE's advantage by roughly a third to a half.
