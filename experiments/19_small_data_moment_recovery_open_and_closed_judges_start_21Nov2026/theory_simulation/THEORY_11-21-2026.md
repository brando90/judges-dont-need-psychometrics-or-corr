**TLDR:** Population excess cross-entropy bounds error in every bounded conditional moment. Mean-based Pearson leaves the mean's scale and offset undetermined and cannot distinguish distributions with the same conditional mean. Neither property orders finite-sample estimation errors. In an exactly solvable example, the amount of data and how frequently the Pearson tie rule is right determine which procedure has lower variance error.

## Cross-entropy and conditional moments

Let $p_x$ be the true distribution of an individual rating $R\in\{0,\dots,5\}$ at input $x$, and $q_x$ a forecast. Individual-rating cross-entropy (CE) loss is $-\log q_x(R)$ in nats, with smaller values better. The population CE of $q$ minus the population CE of $p$ equals $\mathbb E_X D_{\rm KL}(p_X\Vert q_X)$, where $D_{\rm KL}$ denotes Kullback–Leibler divergence. This is zero only when the conditional probabilities agree almost surely. The true law must be representable to achieve zero excess. [Gneiting and Raftery on proper scoring rules](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)

For any bounded rating function $f$ with range width $\Delta_f=\max f-\min f$, write $m_f(q,x)=\sum_r f(r)q_x(r)$. The positive and negative parts of $q_x-p_x$ each sum in magnitude to the total variation distance $\operatorname{TV}(p_x,q_x)=\tfrac12\sum_r|p_x(r)-q_x(r)|$. Subtracting $\min f$ from $f$ gives the first inequality below. Pinsker's inequality in natural-log units gives the second:

$$
|m_f(q,x)-m_f(p,x)|
\le \Delta_f\operatorname{TV}(p_x,q_x)
\le \Delta_f\sqrt{D_{\rm KL}(p_x\Vert q_x)/2}.
$$

For completeness, take the event $A=\{r:p_x(r)>q_x(r)\}$, $s=p_x(A)$ and $u=q_x(A)$. The log-sum inequality gives $D_{\rm KL}(p_x\Vert q_x)\ge s\log(s/u)+(1-s)\log((1-s)/(1-u))$. The right-hand side and its first derivative in $s$ vanish at $s=u$. Its second derivative is $1/[s(1-s)]\ge4$, giving a lower bound $2(s-u)^2=2\operatorname{TV}^2$ by integration. Endpoint cases follow by limits.

Squaring and averaging the moment bound gives

$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_X[(m_f(q,X)-m_f(p,X))^2]
\le \frac{\Delta_f^2}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{CE}(q)-\mathrm{CE}(p)\bigr).}
$$

For $f(r)=r^j$, the width is $5^j$. For the normalized raw moment $f(r)=(r/5)^j$ every positive integer $j$ has width one. For variance, use $\operatorname{Var}_{p_x}(R)=\min_{a\in[0,5]} \mathbb E_{p_x}[(R-a)^2]$. Every loss $(R-a)^2$ in the minimum has width at most $25$. Comparing at the minimizer of each distribution in turn yields $|\operatorname{Var}_{p_x}R-\operatorname{Var}_{q_x}R|\le25\operatorname{TV}(p_x,q_x)$. Its integrated squared error is at most $625/2$ times excess CE. These bounds apply also conditional on a training sample, for its fitted judge, with expectations on independent test draws. Loose upper bounds cannot rank two judges on an individual moment. Empirical train CE can be small while population CE is large.

## What mean-based Pearson can identify

Write $\mu(X)=\mathbb E_p[R\mid X]$ and $m_q(X)=\mathbb E_q[R\mid X]$. The map $q_x\mapsto m_q(x)$ is linear in the six probabilities. With nonconstant $\mu,m_q$ and finite positive rating variance,

$$
\operatorname{Corr}(m_q(X),R)=
\operatorname{Corr}(m_q(X),\mu(X))
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\mu(X))/\operatorname{Var}(R)}.
$$

This follows by conditioning $\operatorname{Cov}(m_q(X),R)$ on $X$. Positive affine means $m_q=a+b\mu$, $b>0$, achieve the same upper bound when feasible. A scalar judge needs level and scale information to identify the conditional mean in rating points. A distribution judge additionally needs information about shape. Among six positive probabilities, four independent directions preserve normalization and the mean. The input-to-rating function can still be nonlinear. [Sample loss, gradient and population derivation](../../18_when_does_ce_beat_correlation_notebook_page_math_and_sweep_start_21Nov2026/conditional_shape_and_rating_budget/MATH_11-21-2026.md)

The finite example here deliberately gives the learner only two candidate mean curves and fixes their scale and offset. They have different shapes across inputs. Pearson can distinguish these curves from training ratings when there is variation. Providing this information gives mean-based Pearson a fair chance to recover the numeric means. No number of training ratings resolves its exact ties between two distribution shapes at each curve. A shape supplied by a model constraint, a prior, a tie rule or a separately trained calibrator changes the procedure.

## A finite-sample variance-risk threshold

In this experiment $R=2+D$ and $D\in\{-1,0,+1\}$. At a given input, the mean of $D$ is $m$ and its second moment about zero is $t$. The three probabilities are $((t-m)/2,1-t,(t+m)/2)$. The two spread values are $t_L=0.4$ and $t_H=0.8$. The absolute variance difference for the same conditional mean is $\delta=t_H-t_L=0.4$, since $\operatorname{Var}(R\mid x)=t-m(x)^2$.

For a supplied, correct mean curve, CE makes an error about spread with probabilities $e_L(N)$ on low-spread truth and $e_H(N)$ on high-spread truth, for $N$ total training ratings. Suppose high spread occurs with prior probability $\pi$, independently of the equiprobable mean curves. CE here uses maximum likelihood, with no prior adjustment. A Pearson selector that always chooses low spread among its tied shapes makes an error with probability $\pi$. Its mean squared error in conditional variance is $\delta^2\pi$. The CE selector's error is $\delta^2[(1-\pi)e_L(N)+\pi e_H(N)]$. Therefore CE has lower expected variance error exactly when

$$
\boxed{\pi>\frac{e_L(N)}{1+e_L(N)-e_H(N)}}.
$$

The denominator is positive in every enumerated setting. The prior describes how often the two training-and-deployment worlds occur, with the same world used for an entire training set and its test ratings. Changing that prior poses a different decision problem. It is distinct from the per-rating probability of drawing 1, 2, or 3. Choosing a high-first Pearson tie gives error probability $1-\pi$, and a fair random tie gives $1/2$. This threshold is specific to this candidate family, training design and tie rule. [Exact wrong-choice probabilities over all budgets](known_mean_prior_threshold.csv)

For one training rating in total, the CE rule with known mean always picks low spread for $R=2$ and high spread for $R=1$ or $3$. Hence $e_L=0.4$ and $e_H=0.2$. The crossover is exactly $1/3$. Sample Pearson is always undefined with a single observation. Its stated fallback supplies its entire forecast in that boundary case. The main comparison starts with three ratings, one at each input type.
