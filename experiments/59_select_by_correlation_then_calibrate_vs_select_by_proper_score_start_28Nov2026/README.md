## Question
The natural objection to the paper is that recalibration removes the gap, so a practitioner can pick the judge by correlation and then recalibrate. With the same few human labels spent either way, does choosing the judge by a proper score do better than choosing it by correlation?
Five frozen frontier judges, five rating sets, label budgets of 25, 50, 100 and 200 single human ratings (one rating per labeled item), 200 random pool/test splits per cell. Regret of a path = the calibrated test risk of the judge it chose minus the calibrated test risk of the best judge for that labeled set,
divided by the base-rate risk, so it is in skill units; this document reports it in percentage points of skill (regret times 100; 0 means the path chose the best judge). The test risk is the unbiased per-sample proper risk on held-out test items: Brier score on the NLI sets, normalized ranked probability score (RPS) on DICES.

## What was found (answer first): the pre-registered hypotheses are falsified, and the data point the other way
1. **Correlation-then-calibrate beat cross-validated proper-score selection in every cell.** The paired difference "regret of path A minus regret of path B" (negative means selecting by correlation is better) is negative in all 20 cells and its 95% interval lies entirely below zero in 17 of them. At n = 25 it is -3.55 (SNLI), -2.37 (MNLI-m), -1.13 (alphaNLI), -6.19 (DICES-350) and -5.46 points (DICES-990);
   at the largest budget it is -0.10 (SNLI), -0.12 (MNLI-m), -0.09 (alphaNLI), -0.23 (DICES-350, 175 items) and -1.02 points (DICES-990); the interval includes zero for SNLI, MNLI-m and alphaNLI and excludes it for DICES-350 (-0.45 to -0.01) and DICES-990. H1 (proper-score selection lower in at least three of five sets at n = 25) is falsified, with zero sets.
   H2 (the two paths within 0.5 points at n = 200 in every set) is falsified by DICES-990 alone, where correlation selection is better by 1.02 points [0.79, 1.25]; H3 (the smallest budget with equivalence in every set) is "none" on the grid.
2. **With 25 labels no path finds the best judge.** The share of draws in which a path picks the best judge is 0.21 to 0.30 for correlation and 0.08 to 0.19 for path B at n = 25 (chance is 0.20); at n = 200 it rises to 0.30 to 0.78 for correlation (0.23 on DICES-350 with its 175 labels).
   The mean regret of correlation selection falls from 5 to 8 points at n = 25 to 0.3 to 1.5 points at the largest budget. At n = 25 the best calibrated judge on the DICES sets even has negative mean skill (-0.035 and -0.012): calibrating on 25 single ratings costs more than it gives, as Experiment 56 found.
3. **Sensitivity, not pre-registered as a decision: selecting by the raw proper score (path C, no calibration inside the selection) is the best or tied path.** It has lower regret than correlation in all eight DICES cells (difference 0.21 to 1.74 points with intervals above zero; for example 0.45 against 1.12 at n = 100 on DICES-990),
   and is within noise of correlation in 9 of the 12 NLI cells (correlation is better on alphaNLI at n = 100 and 200, by 0.22 and 0.52 points; path C is better on MNLI-m at n = 50, by 0.57 points with interval 0.01 to 1.14). It beats path B in 15 of 20 cells. So the cost lies in estimating a calibrator for every candidate on a handful of single ratings inside the selection, not in using a proper score.
4. **What this means for the paper.** Do not claim that choosing among a few judges with a proper score beats choosing by correlation: on these five judges and with at most 200 single ratings the pre-registered comparison goes the other way. The claims the data do support are about reporting (Experiments 57 and 58): correlation misses calibration error and level, and a raw proper score exposes them.
   A plausible reason for this result, not tested here, is that on DICES one judge (Opus) is clearly best by every raw criterion, which both correlation and the raw proper score find with few labels, while the differences in calibrated risk that path B must resolve are small next to the noise of single ratings.

## Method in one paragraph
Per draw (seed `SeedSequence([20261128, 59, set_index, n_index, draw])`): the groups of a set are split at random, half into a pool P and the rest into a test set T (SNLI items that share an image share a group). n items are drawn from P and one rating of each is drawn at random from that item's ratings; these are the labeled items L (n is capped at the pool size, which only matters for the n = 200 cell of DICES-350: 175 items, amendment A1).
Candidate judges are the five frozen judges. Path A picks the judge with the highest Pearson correlation between its expected label value and the single ratings of L; path B picks the judge with the lowest three-fold cross-validated calibrated proper risk on L; path C (sensitivity) picks the lowest raw proper risk on L. The chosen judge is calibrated on all of L by the Experiment 46 map and scored on T using all ratings of each test item.
The oracle is the judge with the lowest calibrated test risk when every judge is calibrated on the same L, so at small n it also profits from favorable calibration fits. Uncertainty: percentile 95% intervals from 4,000 bootstrap draws over the 200 splits (paired across paths); they cover variation over labeled pools given the finite set of items, not sampling of items from a population.

## Scope and limits
Five judges at temperature 0 and five sets; results are specific to a pool in which one judge is clearly best by raw criteria on DICES. Path B is one specific proper-score selector (three-fold calibrated risk on single ratings), and a different one (path C) behaves differently, which is why the sensitivity is reported; a better selector designed after seeing these results would need its own pre-registration.
The regret differences are paired and exclude no draw. No Lean claim is made here (the experiment is empirical).
