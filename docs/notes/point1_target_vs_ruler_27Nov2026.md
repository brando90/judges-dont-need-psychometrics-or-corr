# Point 1: the target is a distribution; what we add is the ruler

**Doc link:** <https://github.com/brando90/judges-dont-need-psychometrics-or-corr/blob/main/docs/notes/point1_target_vs_ruler_27Nov2026.md>

**TLDR:** Pavlick and Kwiatkowski (2019) and Plank (2022) established the target: a human label is a distribution. We adopt that and do not claim it. Our contribution is the ruler: how a judge should be scored once the target is a distribution, and whether current practice scores it that way. It has four parts: a measured practice gap, an identification result, a protocol, and evidence. Real-judge rank reversal is established only narrowly (Experiment 46, interim: none under the pre-registered Pearson test, two Spearman reversals for one judge pair), so we claim only that. We concede the two points of the advisor's Point 1 (define the rater pool and rubric up front; on consensus items a peaked judge is correct) and softened the appendix wording to "cannot identify the distribution in general".

## The short answer

Pavlick and Kwiatkowski, and Plank, argue that a human label is a distribution and that models should be judged against that distribution. That is a claim about the target, and it is not ours. Our claim is about the ruler. Once you accept the target, how should a judge be scored, and does the field score it that way?

Four things are new on top of those papers, and one of them is still open.

First, a measured practice gap. In the forty-nine-paper audit (Experiment 43, a purposive sample coded by one person, not a systematic review), forty-three papers report no item-level proper score (forty-four report no item-level strictly proper one under the stricter recode of Experiment 54, which counts the binned Jensen-Shannon divergence of Elangovan et al. as neither item-level nor proper), twenty-nine of the thirty papers that score outputs report correlation, and all thirteen pairwise-judge papers report agreement, accuracy or kappa. The careful sentence is that, in this sample, LLM-judge validation practice did not act on the point that the target is a distribution. An independent, pre-declared census of forty human-validated sources (Experiment 45) reads the headline statistic instead of every statistic reported and finds the same split: a correlation is the headline in sixteen of forty (twelve of fourteen graded-score judges and metrics, none of the twenty pairwise-preference, frontier-lab, retrieval and annotation sources), and one of forty scores the human rating distribution with a proper rule, at the system level. So correlation is the usual headline for graded-score judges and leaderboard validation, and agreement, accuracy, F1 or kappa for the rest; what they share is a point statistic against one aggregated label.

Second, an identification result. Squared correlation is one minus the squared error of the best affine refit divided by the variance of the rating, and forecasts that share a conditional mean tie on Pearson, Spearman, Kendall and scalar squared error whatever the human distribution is (these statistics see a forecast only through its mean), while every strictly proper score ranks the true distribution strictly ahead of any other forecast that shares its mean. With two categories the mean fixes the distribution, so the tie needs three or more. This is machine-checked in Lean for finite laws, together with the sampled-score result that a collapsed judge can out-correlate a truthful judge that samples from the true distribution. None of it says correlation is always wrong; it says correlation cannot identify the distribution in general.

Third, a protocol. Score with a strictly proper score matched to the label encoding: one-hot Brier or cross-entropy for categorical labels, cumulative RPS for ordinal ones, CRPS or log-density for continuous ones. Compute it per sample on held-out individual ratings, which is unbiased with one rating per item. Report a base-rate skill score and stratify by human agreement. The skill ratio is consistent, not exactly unbiased.

Fourth, evidence that the choice of ruler changes what you see. On seven real rating sets a cross-entropy-trained linear head beats a Pearson-trained one by up to 0.1282 normalized RPS, and at most 0.0024 remains once each head has its own validation-fitted calibration map (Experiment 41). That closure is in-domain: with 25 single held-out ratings the calibrated Pearson-trained head is still worse than the raw cross-entropy-trained head by 0.0097 to 0.0225 normalized RPS on all 16 views (Experiment 47), and a map fitted on another dataset or rubric reopens the gap in 53 of 82 (judge, source, target) cases (five frozen frontier judges and one G-Eval judge; 33 of the 53 move a map between NLI and DICES, which differ in label set; Experiment 48), so each new target needs its own held-out ratings scored with a proper rule. Stratified by human agreement (Experiment 50), the skill of frontier judges over the base rate falls from 0.16 to 0.91 in the highest-agreement fifth of items to between minus 0.38 and 0.11 in the lowest, while their Pearson correlation on the contested NLI items stays between 0.38 and 0.86. On real frontier judges (Experiment 46, interim), four of five judges on DICES-350 are significantly worse than the base rate before calibration (skill in normalized ranked probability score, minus 0.177 to minus 0.096) at 80 to 82 percent top-1 agreement with the human majority. The pre-registered primary test, Pearson correlation against the proper score on the frontier panel, finds no significant rank reversal, and neither do top-1 agreement or kappa. Spearman correlation and the Brier score reverse for one judge pair on two sets (GPT-6 Astra above GLM 5.3 on Spearman by 0.021 on SNLI and 0.034 on MNLI, but lower in Brier skill by 0.039 and 0.098), and no reversal remains after per-judge calibration on held-out ratings. A sensitivity run at B = 20,000 bootstrap draws (a labelled deviation from the frozen B = 4,000) keeps those two frontier reversals (Holm-adjusted p 0.0099 and 0.028) and adds two pairs among small local judges, one of them also on Pearson; both pairs involve a 0.5-billion-parameter model whose verbalized answers are invalid on 52 to 60 percent of items, so they partly contrast confidently wrong with uninformative forecasts. What is open: the local-judge and API panels, which are incomplete.

The pasted draft ("neither paper says anything about it") is too absolute, and four of its sentences need correcting; see the next section.

## Corrections to the pasted draft

These are the places where the draft says more than we can support. Each is fixed in the paper text, or the paper never made the claim.

1. **"Neither paper says anything about it."** Pavlick and Kwiatkowski and Plank do not state an identification result about correlation or squared error. But nearby work evaluates against distributions: Nie et al. (EMNLP 2020, ChaosNLI), Uma et al. (JAIR 2021), Elangovan et al. (ICLR 2025, "Beyond Correlation"), Lyu, Do and Pergola (arXiv 2610.37868) and Li et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2026). The claim we can defend is that the identification statement, in terms of encodings and with a machine check, is ours; the observation that distributions matter is not.
2. **"Correlation, MSE and agreement provably can't tell apart judges with the same conditional mean."** Agreement is wrong in that list. Agreement with the majority label depends on a forecast only through its mode, so it ties forecasts with the same mode, not the same mean. Same-mean forecasts can have different modes. Both statements are checked: the mode statement in core Lean for any number of categories (`Point1.agrees_iff_mode`, `Point1.agreement_depends_only_on_mode`), the same-mean counterexample as a closed core-Lean instance (`Point1.same_mean_different_agreement_instance`, added by the independent QA), and both by exact fractions (claims C2 and C4 in the numerics output).
3. **"Every strictly proper score can separate them."** Strict propriety means the true distribution is the unique minimizer of the expected score. It does not mean two different wrong forecasts always differ: two forecasts at equal distance from the truth can tie on Brier. The paper now says the true distribution scores strictly better than any other forecast that shares its mean (`1-abstract.tex`, `2-introduction.tex`, `3-problem.tex`, `11-supplementary-related-work.tex`; the introduction wording was corrected by the independent QA).
4. **"Machine-checked in Lean" for the whole set.** Lean covers the correlation and scalar-MSE ties, strict propriety of Brier and RPS, the scalar two-category versus three-category contrast, the identity for squared correlation, and both forms of the sampled-score result for finite joint laws. Cross-entropy strict propriety is cited (Gibbs), not formalized. Accuracy against the majority label being proper but not strictly proper, and anything about kappa, is argued by hand.
5. **"A skill score that's unbiased with one rating per item."** The per-sample risk estimate is unbiased. The skill score is a ratio of two risk estimates, so it is consistent, and when the base rate is estimated from the same ratings it has a bias of order one over n (Experiment 52, synthetic, normalized RPS, judge J1: about minus 0.034 at 25 items, minus 0.0017 at 500 and minus 0.0006 at 1,000, with one rating per item; essentially none with a known base rate).
6. **"The field didn't act on their point."** Scope it: LLM-judge validation practice in a convenience sample of forty-nine papers. We did not survey the human label variation literature itself, which does evaluate against distributions.
7. **"Real-judge rank reversal is running now."** Partly established, and narrower than the draft suggests. Experiment 46 (interim) finds no significant reversal under its pre-registered primary test (Pearson against the proper score) or under top-1 agreement or kappa on the frontier panel. It finds two Holm-significant Spearman reversals, both for one pair: GPT-6 Astra is ahead of GLM 5.3 on Spearman by 0.021 on SNLI and 0.034 on MNLI but lower in Brier skill by 0.039 and 0.098, and no reversal remains after per-judge calibration. A sensitivity run at B = 20,000 bootstrap draws (a labelled deviation from the frozen B = 4,000) keeps those two frontier reversals (Holm-adjusted p 0.0099 and 0.028) and adds two pairs among small local judges, one of them also on Pearson; both pairs involve a 0.5-billion-parameter model whose verbalized answers are invalid on 52 to 60 percent of items, so they partly contrast confidently wrong with uninformative forecasts. The Experiment 46 README reads this as "H1 supported"; the frozen H1 names Pearson, so the Spearman result is outside H1 as written, and I report it as a Spearman result. The local-judge panel is partial and the API panel has not run. The abstract and introduction say "Spearman"; the supplement states the Pearson null.

## What we conceded, and where it now lives

**The rater pool and the rubric are part of the construct.** The target is written p*(r | x, P, rho), the distribution of a rating of input x from a stated rater pool P under a stated rubric rho. Changing the pool or the rubric can change p*, so that choice is the construct, fixed before any score is computed. It is stated in the paper's introduction (paragraph "Target versus ruler") and in the problem section (paragraph "The target is set by a rater pool and a rubric"), and in the experiments in the Experiment 44 README and the root README.

What the pool experiment found (Experiment 44, Arm P, DICES, demographic sub-pools of 11 to 139 raters): the item-level rating distributions of two pools differ by a mean total variation distance of 0.08 to 0.18, but so do two random rater groups. No contrast is distinguishable from a rater-label permutation null (smallest Holm-adjusted p 0.59 over six contrasts), no judge-by-contrast score difference is Holm-significant (0 of 30), and the best and second-best judge keep their places in all six contrasts. The hypotheses that the pool changes the verdict (H-P1, H-P2) were **not supported**. That is a statement about power and about asking judges to forecast a whole crowd, not evidence that the pool never matters, so the construct statement stays a requirement of the method, not a finding.

**On consensus items p* is peaked, and a good judge should be too.** That is why the protocol stratifies by human agreement, with agreement measured on one half of each item's ratings and the score computed on the other half. What the data say is narrower than "judges should be peaked on consensus items":

- Experiment 44, Arm S: sharpening a forecast (temperature 0.5) helps relatively more, or hurts less, on consensus items than on contested ones in 25 of 25 judge-by-set cells (interval excludes 0 in all 25), but it improved the consensus stratum in only 5 of 25 cells; on contested items the softest temperature (2) was best in 23 of 25. These judges are overconfident where raters disagree.
- Experiment 50: the raw training-objective gap (Pearson-trained versus cross-entropy-trained head) is larger on high-agreement items, the opposite of our frozen prediction R1 (held on 2 of 12 views). We therefore do not claim correlation-trained judges fail mainly on ambiguous items.

**Softened wording.** The abstract was rewritten later the same day, to the advisor's notes S1 to S9, and no longer contains the "cannot validate a judge" sentence; its remaining impossibility-type sentences are scoped (same-mean distributions tie on correlation by construction; the sampled-score result is stated for scores sampled at nonzero temperature). The problem section now says that none of the statistics identifies the full conditional distribution in general, that this is not a claim that correlation is always misleading, and that with two categories the mean fixes the distribution. No sentence in the paper says correlation is always wrong or that an impossibility holds on every item.
