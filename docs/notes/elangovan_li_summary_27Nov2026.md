# Elangovan et al. and Li et al.: what each does and what it leaves open (27 Nov 2026)

Written for the advisor after reading both papers in full: Elangovan et al., "Beyond Correlation", ICLR 2025 (arXiv:2410.03775v3, 30 pages, read from the PDF) and Li et al., "Judging with Confidence: Calibrating Autoraters to Preference Distributions" (arXiv:2510.00263v1, 38 pages, read from the PDF; cited in the paper as Findings of EMNLP 2026). These are the two papers in `00_main.bib` (`elangovan2025beyond`, `li2026judging`) and in the Experiment 43/54 audit. Section, equation and page numbers below are from those arXiv versions.

## The paragraph (send as is)

Elangovan et al. (ICLR 2025) show that when many items have split human labels, LLM judges can match or beat human–human agreement with the human majority, yet fall well short on items where humans agree (Sec. 3.1, Table 1). They propose stratifying by agreement and a binned Jensen–Shannon divergence: bin items by the human median or majority label, pool all human ratings and sampled judge labels per bin, and average the per-bin divergences (Sec. 3.2, Eqs. 1–2). That is a distributional comparison at bin level; they note binning can hide item-level disagreements and leave significance testing to future work (Secs. 3.2, 4.2). Li et al. (2026) fine-tune pairwise autoraters to state P(B ≻ A), by supervised fine-tuning on ten-vote soft labels or RL with Brier or log rewards, and score them by mean squared error to each item's ten-vote mean, an item-level comparison with the rating distribution (Secs. 3, 5.1). The votes come from Gemini-2.5-Flash with personas, not people (Sec. 4); on human data they report majority agreement and an aggregate win rate (Sec. 5.3, App. D.1); Likert ratings are future work (App. E). So both papers already evaluate judges against the rating distribution. What they leave open, and we take up: strictly proper item-level scores for ordinal or nominal human ratings (also used concurrently by Lyu et al.), estimable from one rating per item; why correlation on sampled scores prefers a collapsed judge; significance-tested Spearman-versus-skill reversals among frontier judges; and recalibration's cost in labels and under shift.

## Pinned claims (what each sentence rests on)

Elangovan et al., arXiv:2410.03775v3:

1. *Split labels flatter judges; agreed items expose them.* Abstract (p. 1): "when the proportion of samples with variation or uncertainty in human assigned labels is relatively high, machine labels ... may superficially appear to have similar or better correlation with the human majority label compared to the human-to-human (HH) correlation ... as the proportion of samples with consistent human labels increases, the correlation between machine and human labels fall well below HH correlation." Evidence: Sec. 3.1, Table 1 (MNLI, SNLI), Table 2 (SummEval), Table 3 (MT-Bench), Fig. 1 (pp. 4–7).
2. *Stratify by agreement.* Sec. 3.1 (p. 4): "we stratify samples and then compare HH and Human-to-machine (HM) correlation measurements for each group. We primarily stratify by percentage agreement"; Sec. 4.3 recommendation 1 (p. 10): "we strongly recommend stratifying results by uncertainty proportions."
3. *Binned JSD definition.* Sec. 3.2, Eq. 1 (p. 7): per-bin pooled PMFs $P_r^h(c)=\sum_{i\in B_r}\sum_{l\in H_i}1\{l=c\}/\sum_{i\in B_r}|H_i|$ and the same for machine labels $M_i$; "for ordinal values, bin $B_r := \{i \mid \mathrm{median}(H_i)=r\}$ ... For nominal perception-based preference labels, bin $B_r := \{i \mid \mathrm{mostfrequent}(H_i)=r\}$". Eq. 2: $JS_b=\sum_{r\in R}\frac{|B_r|}{n}JS(P_r^h\|P_r^m)$, with JS in log base 2. "Sampled judge labels": Sec. 3 (p. 4): "we set the sampling temperature to 1.0 ... We sample 20 machine labels per example."
4. *It is a distributional comparison.* Sec. 3.2 (p. 7): binned JSD "measures how closely machine labels mimic human perception without the need for a single gold label."
5. *Bin level, and why.* Sec. 3.2 (p. 7): "the proposed approach can be applied at item-level when the number of labels collected per item is high enough to form a distribution, however, for judging model generated output even obtaining 3 human labels per item can be expensive. Hence, we suggest computing the distribution distance per bin. The main shortcoming of this approach is that some key item level discrepancies between machines and humans ratings might not be clearly surfaced."
6. *Significance testing left open.* Sec. 4.2 (p. 10): "statistical analysis, such as null-hypothesis and significance testing, is essential ... Future studies should explore these problems, including approaches like resampling."
7. *Not a judge ranking study.* Sec. 3 (p. 4): "our experiments are not meant to rank the underlying LJ, but to study the impact of human uncertainty regardless of the LJ." They do observe that rankings move with the metric or reference: Table 2 caption ("LJ ranking ... change depending on reference human"), Table 3 caption ("the Hw Mw IRA can tell an opposite story" to win-rate). So "metric choice can change a judge ranking" is theirs; what we add is a significance-tested reversal between correlation and a proper score (claim narrowed accordingly).
8. *No proper scoring rule.* The paper's metrics are Krippendorff's alpha, Fleiss/Randolph kappa, percentage agreement, Spearman, Kendall and binned JSD (Sec. 3, "Correlation measures", p. 4; Tables 1–3); no Brier, log or ranked probability score appears. JSD is a divergence between pooled histograms, not a scoring rule on an item-level forecast (our Experiment 54 coding).
9. *Nothing on sampled-score correlation.* Machine labels are sampled at temperature 1.0 and summarized by the machine majority or median (Sec. 3, Tables 1–2); the paper does not analyze how correlation behaves when the judge's score is a single sample versus its mean.

Li et al., arXiv:2510.00263v1:

10. *Target is the preference distribution.* Sec. 2.1 (p. 2–3): $Y\mid X=x\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p^*(x))$ and "a probabilistic judge instead predicts the full preference distribution through its Bernoulli parameter, $p_\theta(x)\in[0,1]\approx p^*(x)$." Pairwise only: "We consider the scenario of pairwise judgements" (Sec. 2.1).
11. *Two training methods.* Sec. 3 (p. 4): Setting 1, SFT on the multi-annotator mean $\hat p(x)=\frac1m\sum_j y^{(j)}$; Setting 2, RL "from Binary Labels via Piecewise Proper Rewards", Brier reward Eq. 1 and clipped log reward Eq. 2, objective Eq. 3; Proposition 1 (p. 5): Fisher consistency, the maximizer reports $p^*(x)$.
12. *Evaluation against the rating distribution, item level.* Sec. 5.1, Metrics (p. 7): "its alignment to the target preference distribution, as measured by the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted $p_\theta(x)$ and the high fidelity estimate $\hat p(x)$ of the true preference distribution"; Table 1 reports MSE, agreement, F1, ECE, Brier. Evaluation set: "1K prompts ... each with 10 annotations to form probabilistic labels" (Sec. 4, p. 5).
13. *Their Brier and ECE are against the majority label.* App. C.1 (p. 19): "we first binarize the preference distribution to obtain ground truth preference labels and then measure the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score. Test samples with ground truth label being 'Tie' are skipped." So the distributional comparison in Li et al. is the MSE to the vote mean; the Brier column is a single-label score.
14. *Votes are synthetic.* Sec. 4, Preference Annotation (p. 5): "we employ Gemini-2.5-Flash ... as an advanced teacher to generate pairwise preference labels ... temperature to 1.0 and condition on a randomly sampled persona"; "this yields ∼110K total annotations."
15. *Human data scored by majority agreement.* Sec. 5.3 (p. 9): PandaLM test set, "each independently annotated by three human experts ... we treat the majority vote as the ground truth and report agreement, precision, recall, and F1 score" (Table 3). App. D.1 (p. 20) also compares a predicted win rate with the human win rate on 900 Chatbot Arena prompts (Table 6), an aggregate, not item-level, comparison.
16. *Ordinal ratings left open.* App. E (p. 21): "the general framework and analysis also apply to the pointwise evaluation setting, where the preference takes nominal or ordinal values (e.g., Likert scale) ... We leave a thorough empirical study in this space as future work."
17. *No correlation-vs-proper comparison, no label-budget or shift study of recalibration.* The metrics are MSE, agreement, F1, ECE, Brier, positional consistency (Secs. 5.1–5.4); temperature scaling, contextual and batch calibration appear only as baselines fitted on "a held-out calibration set" (Sec. 5.1, p. 6), with no sweep over the number of calibration labels and no transfer across datasets. They relate their work to Elangovan et al.: "We take the next step by not only evaluating with respect to this uncertainty, but also proposing methods to directly train models to capture it" (Sec. 6, p. 10).

What our side of the last sentence rests on (all committed):

- Strictly proper score matched to the encoding, one rating per item ranks judges: paper Sec. "Evaluate Conditional Probability Forecasts"; Lean files of Experiments 37/53; Experiment 55 rule-to-encoding table.
- Sampled-score anti-properness of correlation: paper contribution 2, Lean-checked (supplementary related work, "What Is New Here", item 2).
- Significance-tested reversals: Experiment 46 README (two Holm-significant Spearman-vs-Brier-skill reversals, GPT-6 Astra vs GLM 5.3 on SNLI and MNLI).
- Recalibration costs: Experiment 56 README (label budget on the same frontier judges) and Experiment 48 README (shift: 78 of 82 cross-dataset maps lose skill against an in-domain map, 33 worse than uncalibrated).

## What we must stop saying

- That no metric compares a judge with the rating distribution. Binned JSD (Elangovan, Eq. 2) and MSE to the vote mean (Li, Sec. 5.1) both do.
- That proper scoring of judges is new. Li et al. train with Brier and log rewards and prove Fisher consistency (Prop. 1).
- That "metric choice changes judge rankings" is new. Elangovan et al. show it (Tables 2–3 captions); our increment is the significance-tested reversal between correlation and a proper score.
