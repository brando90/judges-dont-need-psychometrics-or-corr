## Draft paragraph (Brando: read the eight passages in the reading list, then rewrite in your own words)

Elangovan et al. (ICLR 2025) show that where human labels are split, a judge's agreement with the human majority can match or exceed human–human agreement, yet falls well below it where humans agree (Sec. 3.1, Tables 1–3). They recommend stratifying by human agreement and propose a binned Jensen–Shannon divergence: bin items by the human median or most frequent label, pool the bin's human ratings into one histogram and its sampled judge labels into another, and take the bin-size-weighted mean of the per-bin divergences (Sec. 3.2, Eqs. 1–2). So a judge is already compared with the human rating distribution, at bin level. Left open: an item-level version, which they say needs enough labels per item when even three are costly; item-level discrepancies, which they name as the main shortcoming; and significance testing (Secs. 3.2, 4.2). Li et al. (2026) train pairwise autoraters to state P(B ≻ A), by fine-tuning on ten-vote means or by RL from one binary vote with Brier or clipped-log rewards (Prop. 1), and score them by squared error to each item's ten-vote mean, an item-level, Brier-type comparison (Secs. 3, 5.1). Left open: human rating distributions, since the votes come from Gemini-2.5-Flash with personas (Sec. 4), the reported Brier and ECE use the majority label (App. C.1) and human data are scored by majority agreement and an aggregate win rate (Sec. 5.3, App. D.1); and ordinal ratings (App. E). Lyu et al. have since scored judges with RPS on ordinal human ratings, so we adopt these scores and claim none. Our results are mixed: the preregistered Pearson test found no rank reversal among five frontier judges, and selecting by correlation and then recalibrating had lower mean regret than selecting by a cross-validated proper score in all 20 budget-by-set cells; what a proper score exposed was level (on DICES-350 the five judges forecast unsafe rates of 9 to 24 percent against a human rate of 33 percent). The identities we use are classical.

## Pinned claims (what each sentence rests on)

Elangovan et al., arXiv:2410.03775v3:

1. *Split labels flatter judges; agreed items expose them.* Abstract (p. 1): "when the proportion of samples with variation or uncertainty in human assigned labels is relatively high, machine labels ... may superficially appear to have similar or better correlation with the human majority label compared to the human-to-human (HH) correlation ... as the proportion of samples with consistent human labels increases, the correlation between machine and human labels fall well below HH correlation." Evidence: Sec. 3.1, Table 1 (MNLI, SNLI), Table 2 (SummEval), Table 3 (MT-Bench), Fig. 1 (pp. 4–7).
2. *Stratify by agreement.* Sec. 3.1 (p. 4): "we stratify samples and then compare HH and Human-to-machine (HM) correlation measurements for each group. We primarily stratify by percentage agreement"; Sec. 4.3 recommendation 1 (p. 10): "we strongly recommend stratifying results by uncertainty proportions."
3. *Binned JSD definition.* Sec. 3.2, Eq. 1 (p. 7): per-bin pooled PMFs $P_r^h(c)=\sum_{i\in B_r}\sum_{l\in H_i}1\{l=c\}/\sum_{i\in B_r}|H_i|$ and the same for machine labels $M_i$; "for ordinal values, bin $B_r := \{i \mid \mathrm{median}(H_i)=r\}$ ... For nominal perception-based preference labels, bin $B_r := \{i \mid \mathrm{mostfrequent}(H_i)=r\}$". Eq. 2: $JS_b=\sum_{r\in R}\frac{|B_r|}{n}JS(P_r^h\|P_r^m)$, with JS in log base 2. "Sampled judge labels": Sec. 3 (p. 4): "we set the sampling temperature to 1.0 ... We sample 20 machine labels per example."
4. *It is a distributional comparison.* Sec. 3.2 (p. 7): binned JSD "measures how closely machine labels mimic human perception without the need for a single gold label."
5. *Bin level, and why.* Sec. 3.2 (p. 7): "the proposed approach can be applied at item-level when the number of labels collected per item is high enough to form a distribution, however, for judging model generated output even obtaining 3 human labels per item can be expensive. Hence, we suggest computing the distribution distance per bin. The main shortcoming of this approach is that some key item level discrepancies between machines and humans ratings might not be clearly surfaced."
6. *Significance testing left open.* Sec. 4.2 (p. 10): "statistical analysis, such as null-hypothesis and significance testing, is essential ... Future studies should explore these problems, including approaches like resampling."
7. *Not a judge ranking study.* Sec. 3 (p. 4): "our experiments are not meant to rank the underlying LJ, but to study the impact of human uncertainty regardless of the LJ." They do observe that rankings move with the metric or reference: Table 2 caption ("LJ ranking ... change depending on reference human"), Table 3 caption ("the Hw Mw IRA can tell an opposite story" to win-rate). So "metric choice can change a judge ranking" is theirs; what we add is a preregistered significance test for such reversals (none for Pearson; two for Spearman on one judge pair, gone after calibration), not the observation itself. Two further precedents (checked 28 Nov 2026): the Table 3 caption makes the binned divergence the one metric on which the otherwise leading judge is not ahead, and App. A.8 (Tables 5 and 6) is a hand-built three-item example in which Kendall's tau (0.00 against 0.82) and Spearman's rho (0.00 against 0.87) prefer the poorer model while the binned divergence prefers the better one (0.56 against 0.65).
8. *No proper scoring rule.* The paper's metrics are Krippendorff's alpha, Fleiss/Randolph kappa, percentage agreement, Spearman, Kendall and binned JSD (Sec. 3, "Correlation measures", p. 4; Tables 1–3); no Brier, log or ranked probability score appears. JSD is a divergence between pooled histograms, not a scoring rule on an item-level forecast (our Experiment 54 coding).
9. *Nothing on sampled-score correlation.* Machine labels are sampled at temperature 1.0 and summarized by the machine majority or median (Sec. 3, Tables 1–2); the paper does not analyze how correlation behaves when the judge's score is a single sample versus its mean. Adjacent: the Table 2 caption records an 11-point gap in Spearman correlation (0.49 against 0.60) between median- and mean-aggregated labels.

Li et al., arXiv:2510.00263v1:

10. *Target is the preference distribution.* Sec. 2.1 (p. 2–3): $Y\mid X=x\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p^*(x))$ and "a probabilistic judge instead predicts the full preference distribution through its Bernoulli parameter, $p_\theta(x)\in[0,1]\approx p^*(x)$." Pairwise only: "We consider the scenario of pairwise judgements" (Sec. 2.1).
11. *Two training methods.* Sec. 3 (p. 4): Setting 1, SFT on the multi-annotator mean $\hat p(x)=\frac1m\sum_j y^{(j)}$; Setting 2, RL "from Binary Labels via Piecewise Proper Rewards", Brier reward Eq. 1 and clipped log reward Eq. 2, objective Eq. 3; Proposition 1 (p. 5): Fisher consistency; the maximizer reports $p^*(x)$ under the Brier reward and $\mathrm{clip}(p^*(x),\epsilon,1-\epsilon)$ under the clipped log reward ("up to clipping for the Log reward").
12. *Evaluation against the rating distribution, item level.* Sec. 5.1, Metrics (p. 7): "its alignment to the target preference distribution, as measured by the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted $p_\theta(x)$ and the high fidelity estimate $\hat p(x)$ of the true preference distribution"; Table 1 reports MSE, agreement, F1, ECE, Brier. Evaluation set: "1K prompts ... each with 10 annotations to form probabilistic labels" (Sec. 4, p. 5).
13. *Their Brier and ECE are against the majority label.* App. C.1 (p. 19): "we first binarize the preference distribution to obtain ground truth preference labels and then measure the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score. Test samples with ground truth label being 'Tie' are skipped." So the distributional comparison in Li et al. is the MSE to the vote mean; the Brier column is a single-label score.
14. *Votes are synthetic.* Sec. 4, Preference Annotation (p. 5): "we employ Gemini-2.5-Flash ... as an advanced teacher to generate pairwise preference labels ... temperature to 1.0 and condition on a randomly sampled persona"; "this yields ∼110K total annotations." (5K prompts with ten votes for fine-tuning, 50K with one for RL, 1K with ten for evaluation.) Their stated reason: most existing datasets lack enough multi-rater annotation for reliable probability estimates; they do not check that the teacher votes match a human distribution.
15. *Human data scored by majority agreement.* Sec. 5.3 (p. 9): PandaLM test set, "each independently annotated by three human experts ... we treat the majority vote as the ground truth and report agreement, precision, recall, and F1 score" (Table 3). App. D.1 (p. 20) also compares a predicted win rate with the human win rate on 900 Chatbot Arena prompts (Table 6), an aggregate, not item-level, comparison.
16. *Ordinal ratings left open.* App. E (p. 21): "the general framework and analysis also apply to the pointwise evaluation setting, where the preference takes nominal or ordinal values (e.g., Likert scale) ... We leave a thorough empirical study in this space as future work."
17. *No correlation-vs-proper comparison, and no study of how post-hoc calibration maps transfer or how many labels they need.* The metrics are MSE, agreement, F1, ECE, Brier, positional consistency (Secs. 5.1–5.4). Of the calibration baselines (Sec. 5.1, p. 6), only temperature scaling is fitted on "a held-out calibration set"; contextual calibration uses a content-free probe and batch calibration is "zero-shot, inference-only". They do evaluate their fine-tuned judges out of distribution on human-annotated PandaLM (Sec. 5.3), and Appendix D.2 compares 5K prompts with ten labels against 50K with one under the same RL Brier objective. They relate their work to Elangovan et al.: "We take the next step by not only evaluating with respect to this uncertainty, but also proposing methods to directly train models to capture it" (Sec. 6, p. 10).

Added 28 Nov 2026 (second reviewer, from the same two papers):

18. *Elangovan et al. already examine DICES-350, with one judge.* App. A.12 (Tables 9 and 10): DICES-350 Safety, 350 items, 100 to 150 annotations per item, one judge (Llama-3-70B). Overall Krippendorff's alpha 0.16 between humans against -0.25 for human majority versus machine majority; percentage agreement 0.69 against 0.39; Randolph's kappa 0.35 against 0.09. No binned divergence is reported for DICES, and the authors say the model struggles across all metrics and strata and call for deeper investigation. The reviewer asked once for these values; Brando should read Table 10 himself. Our DICES-350 analysis is the deeper investigation they call for, with a distributional score and five frontier judges.
19. *Where Elangovan's labels are not twenty per item.* The twenty labels per item at temperature 1 are the ones they sample themselves; for the published MT-Bench GPT-4 judgments they reuse there was one per item (their Fig. 4 caption).
20. *Their Eq. 2 and the repo's adaptation.* Their binned divergence pools sampled judge labels. This repository's Experiment 44 adapts it to probabilistic forecasts by pooling the human ratings and comparing them with the mean forecast in each bin. Their Eq. 2 uses base-2 logarithms; their App. A.7 code calls SciPy's `jensenshannon` without a base (read through WebFetch; the SciPy default is from memory), which would return a square-root distance in natural logarithms, so compare magnitudes with care.
21. *Li et al., App. D.2 and the log reward.* RL on 50K prompts with one label each beats SFT on 5K prompts with ten labels on every metric of their Table 1 for the Brier reward but not for the log reward (Qwen-2.5-7B: MSE 0.1192 against 0.1143 for SFT). The numbers 0.8575 (agreement) and 0.1015 (ECE) for the 50K model are in their Table 1; the 0.7913 and 0.1321 quoted in the supplement for the 5K-prompt RL model could not be found in the text (App. D.2 gives them in Fig. 3, an image) and are unverified until someone reads the bar labels.
22. *Li et al. leave ordinal scales to future work; Lyu et al. have since taken them up.* App. E asserts that the framework carries over to Likert-type ratings and defers the study. The Lyu et al. statement rests on this repository's reading of that paper, which the second reviewer did not read.

What our side of the closing sentences rests on (all committed):

- A strictly proper score matched to the encoding, with one rating per item, ranks judges: paper Sec. "Evaluate Conditional Probability Forecasts"; Lean files of Experiments 37 and 53; Experiment 55 rule-to-encoding table. One rating per item estimates each judge's expected score without bias (Lean-checked special case); it does not estimate the distance to the true distribution, which also needs the uncertainty term (Experiment 53, `UncertaintyTerm.lean`).
- Sampled-score anti-properness of correlation: paper contribution 2, Lean-checked for finite laws (supplementary related work, "What Is New Here", item 2). The identities are classical (reliability and the index of reliability of classical test theory; Pearson's correlation ratio); what we add is their statement for judge validation.
- Preregistered tests on frozen frontier judges: Experiment 46 (0 of 50 Pearson reversals significant; the two Holm-significant Spearman reversals, GPT-6 Astra against GLM 5.3 on SNLI and MNLI, disappear after calibration; see its erratum of 11-27-2026).
- Level, not rank: Experiment 58 (on DICES-350 the human unsafe rate is 32.7 percent and the judges' mean forecast probabilities are 8.6 to 24.0 percent; a hard-label judge that matched the human majority would itself be 9.8 points below the human rate, Experiment 61).
- Recalibration costs: Experiment 56 (label budget on the same frontier judges; 100 single ratings is the smallest tested budget at which every judge's mean skill is above the base rate) and Experiment 48 (across 82 transfers, 78 significantly below an in-domain map and 33 significantly worse than uncalibrated, 21 of those 33 between NLI and DICES, whose label sets differ).
- Against our own recommendation: Experiment 59 (selecting by correlation and then recalibrating had lower mean regret than selecting by a cross-validated proper score in all 20 budget-by-set cells; 17 with the interval below 0; against a uniformly random pick the proper-score path was worse in 11 of 20 cells, 9 significant, Experiment 61).

## What we must stop saying

- That no metric compares a judge with the rating distribution. Binned JSD (Elangovan, Eq. 2) and MSE to the vote mean (Li, Sec. 5.1) both do.
- That proper scoring of judges is new. Li et al. train with Brier and log rewards and prove Fisher consistency (Prop. 1).
- That "metric choice changes judge rankings" is new. Elangovan et al. show it (Tables 2–3 captions); our increment is a preregistered significance test for such reversals on real judges (none for Pearson; two for Spearman on one judge pair, gone after calibration), not the observation that metrics can disagree.
